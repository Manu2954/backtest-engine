                    FOUNDATIONS - TIME SERIES AND MARKET REGIMES

1.1 INTRODUCTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Before we can detect "regimes" in financial markets, we must establish a
rigorous foundation. This chapter answers the fundamental questions:

    - What is a time series?
    - What makes financial time series special?
    - What do we mean by "regime"?
    - Why does regime detection matter for trading?

Understanding these foundations is critical because many regime detection
failures stem from conceptual misunderstandings, not implementation bugs.


1.2 TIME SERIES: DEFINITION AND PROPERTIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DEFINITION 1.1 (Time Series)
    A time series is a sequence of observations {X_t} indexed by time t,
    where t belongs to some index set T (typically T = {1, 2, 3, ...} for
    discrete time or T = [0, ∞) for continuous time).

In financial markets, our time series are typically:
    - Price series: P_t (e.g., closing prices)
    - Return series: R_t = (P_t - P_{t-1}) / P_{t-1}  (simple returns)
    - Log return series: r_t = log(P_t / P_{t-1})    (log returns)

EXAMPLE 1.1
    Daily closing prices of SPY from 2020-01-01 to 2020-12-31:

    Date        Price       Return      Log Return
    2020-01-02  324.87      --          --
    2020-01-03  322.41      -0.76%      -0.0076
    2020-01-06  321.52      -0.28%      -0.0028
    ...         ...         ...         ...

KEY PROPERTIES OF TIME SERIES:

1. Temporal Ordering: The order matters. Shuffling destroys information.

2. Autocorrelation: Observations may be correlated with their past values.

   DEFINITION 1.2 (Autocorrelation)
       The autocorrelation at lag k is:
       ρ(k) = Corr(X_t, X_{t-k}) = Cov(X_t, X_{t-k}) / Var(X_t)

   Financial returns typically have:
   - Near-zero autocorrelation in returns (weak form efficiency)
   - Positive autocorrelation in squared returns (volatility clustering)

3. Distributional Properties: The distribution of X_t may vary over time.


1.3 STATIONARITY: THE CENTRAL CONCEPT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DEFINITION 1.3 (Strict Stationarity)
    A time series {X_t} is strictly stationary if the joint distribution
    of (X_{t1}, X_{t2}, ..., X_{tk}) is identical to the joint distribution
    of (X_{t1+h}, X_{t2+h}, ..., X_{tk+h}) for all t1, t2, ..., tk and all h.

In plain English: the statistical properties don't change over time.

DEFINITION 1.4 (Weak/Covariance Stationarity)
    A time series {X_t} is weakly stationary if:
    1. E[X_t] = μ for all t (constant mean)
    2. Var(X_t) = σ² for all t (constant variance)
    3. Cov(X_t, X_{t-k}) = γ(k) depends only on k, not on t

Weak stationarity is the practical definition used in most applications.

WHY STATIONARITY MATTERS:

Most statistical models assume stationarity. If a series is non-stationary,
standard methods give misleading results.

EXAMPLE 1.2 (Spurious Results from Non-Stationarity)
    If you regress two independent random walks on each other, you'll
    often find "significant" relationships that don't exist. This is
    the spurious regression problem.

TESTING FOR STATIONARITY:

1. Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) Test
   - H0: Series has a unit root (non-stationary)
   - H1: Series is stationary

2. KPSS Test
   - H0: Series is stationary
   - H1: Series has a unit root

3. Visual inspection: Does the series look like it wanders without bound?


1.4 NON-STATIONARITY IN FINANCIAL MARKETS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Financial price series are almost always non-stationary. This is fundamental.

THEOREM 1.1 (Prices vs Returns)
    If prices follow a random walk: P_t = P_{t-1} + ε_t
    Then prices are non-stationary (variance grows with time)
    But returns r_t = P_t - P_{t-1} = ε_t are stationary (if ε_t is i.i.d.)

This is why we typically work with RETURNS, not prices.

HOWEVER, even returns exhibit non-stationarity in practice:

1. TIME-VARYING VOLATILITY

   Volatility clusters: high volatility tends to follow high volatility.

   EXAMPLE 1.3 (Volatility Clustering)
       March 2020 (COVID crash): Daily SPY volatility ~5%
       July 2020 (recovery):     Daily SPY volatility ~1%

   The variance of returns is NOT constant over time.

2. TIME-VARYING MEAN

   Bull markets have positive mean returns; bear markets have negative.

   EXAMPLE 1.4 (Time-Varying Mean)
       2019 SPY: Mean daily return ≈ +0.12%
       Q1 2020:  Mean daily return ≈ -0.50%

3. TIME-VARYING CORRELATIONS

   Asset correlations increase during crises ("correlation breakdown").

4. STRUCTURAL BREAKS

   Major events (policy changes, crises) can permanently alter behavior.


1.5 WHAT IS A REGIME?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DEFINITION 1.5 (Regime - Informal)
    A regime is a period during which the statistical properties of a
    time series remain approximately constant. A regime change occurs
    when these properties shift to a new stable configuration.

DEFINITION 1.6 (Regime - Formal)
    Let {X_t} be a time series. A regime is characterized by a parameter
    vector θ that determines the distribution of X_t:

        X_t | S_t = j ~ F(θ_j)

    where S_t ∈ {1, 2, ..., K} is the regime indicator at time t, and
    θ_j is the parameter vector for regime j.

EXAMPLE 1.5 (Gaussian Regime Model)
    Suppose returns follow:
        r_t | S_t = j ~ Normal(μ_j, σ_j²)

    With two regimes:
        Regime 1 (Bull):  μ₁ = +0.1%, σ₁ = 1.0%
        Regime 2 (Bear):  μ₂ = -0.2%, σ₂ = 2.5%

    This model captures both the mean shift AND volatility shift
    between bull and bear markets.

KEY INSIGHT: The definition of "regime" depends on WHAT properties you care
about. Different parameterizations lead to different regime definitions.


1.6 TYPES OF REGIMES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Not all regime definitions are equal. The choice depends on your use case.

TYPE 1: TREND REGIMES

Definition: Based on the DIRECTION of price movement.

    BULL:     Sustained upward price movement
    BEAR:     Sustained downward price movement
    SIDEWAYS: No clear directional trend

Mathematical characterization:
    - BULL:     E[r_t] > 0, often with positive slope in prices
    - BEAR:     E[r_t] < 0, often with negative slope in prices
    - SIDEWAYS: E[r_t] ≈ 0, prices oscillate around a mean

Use case: Trend-following strategies care about direction.


TYPE 2: VOLATILITY REGIMES

Definition: Based on the MAGNITUDE of price fluctuations.

    LOW_VOL:  Small daily moves, calm markets
    HIGH_VOL: Large daily moves, turbulent markets

Mathematical characterization:
    - LOW_VOL:  Var(r_t) < threshold
    - HIGH_VOL: Var(r_t) > threshold

Use case: Options pricing, risk management, position sizing.


TYPE 3: MARKET CHARACTER REGIMES

Definition: Based on HOW prices move (trending vs mean-reverting).

    TRENDING:       Momentum persists, autocorrelation > 0
    MEAN_REVERTING: Prices revert to mean, autocorrelation < 0
    RANDOM:         No predictable pattern, autocorrelation ≈ 0

Mathematical characterization:
    - TRENDING:       ρ(1) > 0 (yesterday's return predicts today's)
    - MEAN_REVERTING: ρ(1) < 0 (yesterday's return reverses)
    - RANDOM:         ρ(1) ≈ 0 (no predictability)

Use case: Strategy selection (momentum vs mean-reversion).


TYPE 4: COMPOSITE REGIMES

Real markets exhibit combinations:

    Example: "High-volatility bear market"
    - Trend: BEAR (negative mean return)
    - Volatility: HIGH_VOL (large fluctuations)

    Example: "Low-volatility bull market"
    - Trend: BULL (positive mean return)
    - Volatility: LOW_VOL (steady grind higher)

Our implementation focuses on TREND REGIMES (BULL/BEAR/SIDEWAYS) because
they're most actionable for strategy analysis.


1.7 WHY REGIME DETECTION MATTERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

REASON 1: STRATEGY PERFORMANCE IS REGIME-DEPENDENT

Most trading strategies perform differently across regimes:

    Strategy Type         Best Regime       Worst Regime
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    Trend-following       TRENDING          SIDEWAYS
    Mean-reversion        SIDEWAYS          TRENDING
    Buy-and-hold          BULL              BEAR
    Volatility selling    LOW_VOL           HIGH_VOL

If you don't know which regime you're in, you can't assess strategy fitness.

REASON 2: RISK MANAGEMENT

Risk varies dramatically by regime:

    - Bear markets: Drawdowns are deeper and faster
    - High volatility: Stop-losses trigger more frequently
    - Regime transitions: Maximum danger (model breaks down)

Regime awareness enables adaptive risk management.

REASON 3: AVOIDING OVERFITTING

A strategy that only works in bull markets will fail in bear markets.
Regime-aware backtesting reveals this vulnerability BEFORE live trading.

REASON 4: STRATEGY ALLOCATION

Multi-strategy portfolios can allocate capital based on regime:

    - Regime = BULL: Increase trend-following allocation
    - Regime = SIDEWAYS: Increase mean-reversion allocation
    - Regime = HIGH_VOL: Reduce leverage across all strategies


1.8 THE FUNDAMENTAL TRADE-OFF
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Regime detection faces an unavoidable trade-off:

    SENSITIVITY vs STABILITY

HIGH SENSITIVITY (detect many regime changes):
    ✓ Catches short-term shifts quickly
    ✗ Many false positives (whipsaws)
    ✗ Regimes become too short to be actionable

LOW SENSITIVITY (detect few regime changes):
    ✓ Stable regime labels
    ✓ Fewer false positives
    ✗ Slow to detect actual changes
    ✗ May miss important transitions

There is no "correct" sensitivity level. It depends on:
    - Your trading timeframe
    - Cost of being wrong (false positive vs false negative)
    - Strategy requirements

EXAMPLE 1.6 (Timeframe Dependence)
    A day trader might want regimes that last hours.
    A position trader might want regimes that last months.
    The same price series produces different "correct" regimes!


1.9 MATHEMATICAL FORMALIZATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Let's formalize the regime detection problem.

SETUP:
    - Observed time series: {X_t}_{t=1}^{T}
    - True (unobserved) regime sequence: {S_t}_{t=1}^{T} where S_t ∈ {1,...,K}
    - Regime parameters: {θ_1, ..., θ_K}

GOAL:
    Estimate the regime sequence {Ŝ_t} from observations {X_t}.

APPROACHES:

1. SUPERVISED (if we have labeled regimes):

   Train a classifier: Ŝ_t = f(X_{t-w:t}) where w is a lookback window.

   Problem: We rarely have labeled regime data.

2. UNSUPERVISED (no labels):

   a) Changepoint Detection + Clustering:
      - Step 1: Find times τ₁, τ₂, ... where distribution changes
      - Step 2: Cluster segments into K regime types

   b) Hidden Markov Models:
      - Model regime transitions as Markov chain
      - Learn parameters via EM algorithm
      - Infer most likely regime sequence

3. HYBRID:

   Combine changepoint detection with rule-based classification.

Our implementation uses approach 2a: PELT for changepoints, GMM for clustering.


1.10 CHAPTER SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

KEY CONCEPTS:

1. Time series have temporal structure that must be respected.

2. Financial markets are NON-STATIONARY: means, variances, and correlations
   change over time. This is why regime detection is necessary.

3. A REGIME is a period of approximately constant statistical properties.
   The definition depends on WHICH properties you measure.

4. TREND REGIMES (BULL/BEAR/SIDEWAYS) are based on price direction.
   VOLATILITY REGIMES are based on fluctuation magnitude.
   MARKET CHARACTER REGIMES are based on autocorrelation structure.

5. Regime detection matters because:
   - Strategy performance is regime-dependent
   - Risk varies by regime
   - Regime-aware analysis prevents overfitting

6. The fundamental trade-off is SENSITIVITY vs STABILITY.

2.1 INTRODUCTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Once we accept that markets have regimes, the next question is:

    "Where do the regime boundaries occur?"

This is the CHANGEPOINT DETECTION problem. This chapter develops the theory
from first principles, culminating in the PELT algorithm we use.


2.2 WHAT IS A CHANGEPOINT?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DEFINITION 2.1 (Changepoint)
    A changepoint is a time τ at which the statistical properties of a
    time series change. Formally, if {X_t} has distribution F_1 for t < τ
    and distribution F_2 for t ≥ τ, then τ is a changepoint.

EXAMPLE 2.1 (Mean Shift)
    Consider: X_t = μ_t + ε_t where ε_t ~ N(0, 1)

    If μ_t = 0 for t < 50 and μ_t = 2 for t ≥ 50,
    then τ = 50 is a changepoint in the mean.

    Visually:

    |     ·  ·      · ·   |          ·  · ·  ·
    |  ·    ·    ·        |       ·         ·   ·
    | ·  ·    · ·    ·    |    ·    ·   ·
    |    ·       ·        |  ·         ·    ·
    +─────────────────────+──────────────────────>
                         τ=50                    t

EXAMPLE 2.2 (Variance Shift)
    X_t = ε_t where:
    - ε_t ~ N(0, 1) for t < 50
    - ε_t ~ N(0, 4) for t ≥ 50

    The mean stays at 0, but variance quadruples at τ = 50.

EXAMPLE 2.3 (Multiple Changepoints)
    Real data typically has MULTIPLE changepoints:

    Segment 1: t ∈ [1, τ₁)      properties θ₁
    Segment 2: t ∈ [τ₁, τ₂)    properties θ₂
    Segment 3: t ∈ [τ₂, τ₃)    properties θ₃
    ...

    We need to find ALL changepoints: {τ₁, τ₂, τ₃, ...}


2.3 PROBLEM FORMULATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

FORMAL PROBLEM STATEMENT:

Given:
    - Time series {X_t}_{t=1}^{T}
    - Number of changepoints m (unknown or specified)

Find:
    - Changepoint locations τ = {τ₁, τ₂, ..., τ_m}
    - Such that data is well-described by piecewise-constant parameters

APPROACHES:

1. KNOWN NUMBER OF CHANGEPOINTS:
   Find exactly m changepoints that best explain the data.

2. UNKNOWN NUMBER OF CHANGEPOINTS:
   Find the number m AND locations {τ₁, ..., τ_m} jointly.
   Requires penalizing model complexity.

Real-world regime detection uses approach 2 (unknown number).


2.4 COST FUNCTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We need a way to measure how well a segment fits a model. This is the
COST FUNCTION (also called loss function or objective function).

DEFINITION 2.2 (Segment Cost)
    The cost C(X_{a:b}) measures how poorly data from time a to b
    fits a single-regime model. Lower cost = better fit.

COMMON COST FUNCTIONS:

1. L2 COST (Gaussian assumption)

   C_L2(X_{a:b}) = Σ_{t=a}^{b} (X_t - X̄_{a:b})²

   where X̄_{a:b} = (1/(b-a+1)) Σ_{t=a}^{b} X_t is the segment mean.

   Interpretation: Sum of squared deviations from segment mean.
   Assumes: Data is Gaussian with constant mean within segment.

   EXAMPLE 2.4
       Segment data: [2, 3, 2, 3, 2]
       Mean: 2.4
       L2 cost: (2-2.4)² + (3-2.4)² + (2-2.4)² + (3-2.4)² + (2-2.4)²
              = 0.16 + 0.36 + 0.16 + 0.36 + 0.16 = 1.2

2. L1 COST (Robust to outliers)

   C_L1(X_{a:b}) = Σ_{t=a}^{b} |X_t - median(X_{a:b})|

   Interpretation: Sum of absolute deviations from median.
   More robust to outliers than L2.

3. LIKELIHOOD-BASED COST

   C_LL(X_{a:b}) = -2 · log L(θ̂ | X_{a:b})

   where θ̂ is the MLE for the segment.

   For Gaussian with unknown mean and variance:
   C_LL(X_{a:b}) = n · log(σ̂²) + n
   where σ̂² = (1/n) Σ(X_t - X̄)²

4. RBF (RADIAL BASIS FUNCTION) KERNEL COST

   C_RBF(X_{a:b}) = n - Σ_{i,j} K(X_i, X_j) / n

   where K(x,y) = exp(-γ||x-y||²) is the RBF kernel.

   Non-parametric: doesn't assume specific distribution.
   Good for detecting changes in general distributional shape.

MULTIVARIATE COST:

When X_t is a vector (e.g., [returns, volatility]), extend naturally:

   C_L2(X_{a:b}) = Σ_{t=a}^{b} ||X_t - X̄_{a:b}||²

   where ||·|| is the Euclidean norm.


2.5 THE OPTIMIZATION PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

TOTAL COST:

Given changepoints τ = {τ₁, ..., τ_m}, the total cost is:

   J(τ) = C(X_{1:τ₁}) + C(X_{τ₁+1:τ₂}) + ... + C(X_{τ_m+1:T})
        = Σ_{i=0}^{m} C(X_{τ_i+1:τ_{i+1}})

where τ₀ = 0 and τ_{m+1} = T by convention.

PENALIZED COST:

To avoid overfitting (finding too many changepoints), add a penalty:

   J_pen(τ) = J(τ) + λ · |τ|

where |τ| = m is the number of changepoints and λ is the penalty parameter.

OPTIMIZATION PROBLEM:

   τ* = argmin_τ J_pen(τ) = argmin_τ [Σ_i C(X_{segment_i}) + λ · m]

This balances:
   - Low cost (fits data well) → more changepoints
   - Low penalty (simple model) → fewer changepoints


2.6 PENALTY SELECTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The penalty λ controls the sensitivity-stability trade-off.

GUIDELINES:

   λ too small → Too many changepoints (overfitting)
   λ too large → Too few changepoints (underfitting)

COMMON CHOICES:

1. BIC (BAYESIAN INFORMATION CRITERION)

   λ_BIC = log(T)

   where T is the series length.

   Derivation: Comes from Bayesian model selection, penalizing by
   the number of parameters (proportional to m) scaled by log(T).

2. AIC (AKAIKE INFORMATION CRITERION)

   λ_AIC = 2

   Constant penalty per changepoint. Less conservative than BIC.

3. MODIFIED BIC

   λ_mBIC = c · log(T)

   where c > 1 is a constant (often c = 1.5 or 2).
   More conservative for long series.

4. CROSS-VALIDATION

   - Split data into train/validation
   - Try different λ values
   - Choose λ that minimizes validation cost

   Problem: Computationally expensive; requires careful splitting.

5. ELBOW METHOD

   - Plot number of changepoints vs λ
   - Find "elbow" where adding more changepoints doesn't help much

OUR CHOICE:

We use λ = log(T), the BIC penalty. For T = 1500 bars:

   λ = log(1500) ≈ 7.3

This provides a reasonable balance for daily financial data.


2.7 SOLVING THE OPTIMIZATION: ALGORITHMS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Finding optimal changepoints is computationally challenging. Let's examine
the algorithms from simple to sophisticated.

ALGORITHM 1: BRUTE FORCE

   For each possible set of changepoints τ:
       Compute J_pen(τ)
   Return τ with minimum J_pen

Complexity: O(2^T) - exponential in series length. INFEASIBLE.


ALGORITHM 2: BINARY SEGMENTATION

Idea: Greedily find one changepoint at a time.

   BinarySegmentation(X, λ):
       τ* = argmin_τ [C(X_{1:τ}) + C(X_{τ+1:T})]
       if C(X) - C(X_{1:τ*}) - C(X_{τ*+1:T}) > λ:
           changepoints = {τ*}
           changepoints += BinarySegmentation(X_{1:τ*}, λ)
           changepoints += BinarySegmentation(X_{τ*+1:T}, λ)
           return changepoints
       else:
           return {}

Complexity: O(T log T) average, O(T²) worst case.

Problem:
   - Greedy approach may miss optimal solution
   - Order of detection affects results
   - First changepoint "locks in" and may be suboptimal


ALGORITHM 3: OPTIMAL PARTITIONING (Dynamic Programming)

Idea: Find globally optimal solution via dynamic programming.

Define F(t) = minimum penalized cost for data X_{1:t}.

Recurrence:
   F(t) = min_{s < t} [F(s) + C(X_{s+1:t}) + λ]

with F(0) = -λ (so first segment doesn't get penalized).

To find changepoints, track which s achieved the minimum at each t.

Complexity: O(T²) - quadratic in series length.

Problem: O(T²) is slow for long series (T = 10000 → 100 million operations).


ALGORITHM 4: PELT (Pruned Exact Linear Time)

Idea: Optimal partitioning with pruning to achieve O(T) complexity.

Key insight: Many candidate split points can be PRUNED (eliminated from
consideration) because they can never be optimal.

PRUNING RULE:
   If at time t we have F(s) + C(X_{s+1:t}) > F(t), then s can be
   pruned and never reconsidered for any future time t' > t.

Intuition: If adding more data to segment [s+1, t] makes it worse than
the best segmentation ending at t, then it will only get worse as we
add even more data.

PELT ALGORITHM:

   PELT(X, λ):
       T = length(X)
       F[0] = -λ
       candidates = {0}

       for t = 1 to T:
           # Find best split point
           F[t] = min_{s ∈ candidates} [F[s] + C(X_{s+1:t}) + λ]
           best_s[t] = argmin achieving above

           # Prune candidates
           candidates = {s ∈ candidates : F[s] + C(X_{s+1:t}) ≤ F[t]} ∪ {t}

       # Backtrack to find changepoints
       changepoints = []
       t = T
       while t > 0:
           changepoints.prepend(best_s[t])
           t = best_s[t]

       return changepoints

Complexity: O(T) expected for data with changepoints!

Why O(T)? On average, the number of candidates stays bounded because
pruning removes candidates at roughly the same rate they're added.


2.8 PELT: DETAILED WALKTHROUGH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Let's trace through PELT on a small example.

EXAMPLE 2.5

Data: X = [1, 1, 1, 5, 5, 5]  (mean shift at t=4)
Cost function: L2 (sum of squared deviations from mean)
Penalty: λ = 2

STEP-BY-STEP:

Initialize:
   F[0] = -2  (= -λ)
   candidates = {0}

t = 1:
   Only candidate is s = 0
   F[1] = F[0] + C(X_{1:1}) + λ
        = -2 + 0 + 2 = 0
   best_s[1] = 0
   candidates = {0, 1}

t = 2:
   Candidate s = 0: F[0] + C(X_{1:2}) + λ = -2 + 0 + 2 = 0
   Candidate s = 1: F[1] + C(X_{2:2}) + λ = 0 + 0 + 2 = 2

   F[2] = min(0, 2) = 0
   best_s[2] = 0

   Pruning: F[1] + C(X_{2:2}) = 0 + 0 = 0 ≤ F[2] = 0 ✓ (keep)
   candidates = {0, 1, 2}

t = 3:
   Candidate s = 0: F[0] + C([1,1,1]) + λ = -2 + 0 + 2 = 0
   Candidate s = 1: F[1] + C([1,1]) + λ = 0 + 0 + 2 = 2
   Candidate s = 2: F[2] + C([1]) + λ = 0 + 0 + 2 = 2

   F[3] = 0
   best_s[3] = 0
   candidates = {0, 1, 2, 3}  (all survive pruning)

t = 4:
   Candidate s = 0: F[0] + C([1,1,1,5]) + λ = -2 + 12 + 2 = 12
   Candidate s = 1: F[1] + C([1,1,5]) + λ = 0 + 8 + 2 = 10
   Candidate s = 2: F[2] + C([1,5]) + λ = 0 + 8 + 2 = 10
   Candidate s = 3: F[3] + C([5]) + λ = 0 + 0 + 2 = 2

   F[4] = 2
   best_s[4] = 3  ← Changepoint detected!

   Pruning: s=0,1,2 all have cost > F[4], prune them
   candidates = {3, 4}

t = 5:
   Candidate s = 3: F[3] + C([5,5]) + λ = 0 + 0 + 2 = 2
   Candidate s = 4: F[4] + C([5]) + λ = 2 + 0 + 2 = 4

   F[5] = 2
   best_s[5] = 3
   candidates = {3, 5}

t = 6:
   Candidate s = 3: F[3] + C([5,5,5]) + λ = 0 + 0 + 2 = 2
   Candidate s = 5: F[5] + C([5]) + λ = 2 + 0 + 2 = 4

   F[6] = 2
   best_s[6] = 3

BACKTRACK:
   t = 6: best_s[6] = 3 → changepoint at 3
   t = 3: best_s[3] = 0 → stop

   Changepoints = {3}  (meaning segment 1-3, segment 4-6)

This correctly identifies the mean shift between t=3 and t=4!


2.9 MINIMUM SEGMENT LENGTH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

An important practical constraint is the MINIMUM SEGMENT LENGTH.

MOTIVATION:

1. Statistical reliability: Very short segments have unreliable statistics.
   With only 5 data points, you can't estimate mean/variance well.

2. Actionability: A "regime" lasting 3 days isn't useful for most strategies.

3. Overfitting prevention: Without minimum length, PELT might fit noise.

IMPLEMENTATION:

Modify PELT to only consider candidates s where t - s ≥ min_length:

   candidates = {s : t - s ≥ min_length and F[s] + C(X_{s+1:t}) ≤ F[t]}

OUR CHOICE:

We use min_segment_length = 20 bars (about one trading month for daily data).


2.10 ONLINE VS OFFLINE DETECTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OFFLINE DETECTION (what we use):

   - Have ALL data available
   - Find changepoints retrospectively
   - Can look at future data when detecting past changepoints
   - Better accuracy, but not real-time

ONLINE DETECTION:

   - Data arrives sequentially
   - Detect changepoints as they happen
   - Cannot look at future data
   - Lower accuracy, but real-time

EXAMPLE 2.6 (Online vs Offline)

   Data: [1, 1, 1, 3, 3, 3, 3, 3]

   Offline: Immediately identifies changepoint at t=4
   Online:  At t=4, sees [1,1,1,3]. Is this a changepoint or noise?
            At t=5, sees [1,1,1,3,3]. More confident...
            At t=7, sees [1,1,1,3,3,3,3]. Now confident!

   Online detection has DELAY.

ONLINE ALGORITHMS:

1. BOCPD (Bayesian Online Changepoint Detection)
   - Maintains probability distribution over run lengths
   - Updates probabilities with each new observation
   - Declares changepoint when probability exceeds threshold

2. CUSUM (Cumulative Sum Control Chart)
   - Tracks cumulative deviation from expected value
   - Signals change when cumulative sum exceeds threshold

For BACKTESTING, offline detection is appropriate because we have all
historical data. For LIVE TRADING, online detection would be needed.


2.11 CHAPTER SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

KEY CONCEPTS:

1. A CHANGEPOINT is where statistical properties shift.

2. We find changepoints by minimizing PENALIZED COST:
   J_pen(τ) = Σ_i C(segment_i) + λ · |τ|

3. The PENALTY λ controls sensitivity vs stability trade-off.
   - BIC penalty: λ = log(T)
   - Higher λ → fewer changepoints

4. COST FUNCTIONS measure segment homogeneity:
   - L2: Sum of squared deviations (Gaussian assumption)
   - RBF: Kernel-based (non-parametric)

5. PELT algorithm achieves O(T) complexity through pruning:
   - Exact solution (not approximate)
   - Prunes candidates that can never be optimal

6. MINIMUM SEGMENT LENGTH prevents overfitting and ensures
   statistical reliability.

CRITICAL INSIGHT:

PELT finds changepoints in WHATEVER SIGNAL you give it. If you give it:
   - Returns → finds return distribution changes
   - Volatility → finds volatility changes
   - Price → finds price level changes

The signal design (Chapter 3) is just as important as the algorithm.


================================================================================
                                  CHAPTER 3
                     SIGNAL DESIGN FOR REGIME DETECTION
================================================================================

3.1 INTRODUCTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Chapter 2 showed HOW to find changepoints. This chapter addresses the
equally important question: changepoints in WHAT?

The SIGNAL you feed to PELT determines what kind of regime changes you detect.
This is perhaps the most underappreciated aspect of regime detection.

CRITICAL PRINCIPLE:

    PELT finds changepoints where the SIGNAL's statistical properties change.
    It knows nothing about "bull markets" or "bear markets" - only numbers.


3.2 THE SIGNAL CHOICE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Consider detecting regime changes in SPY during 2018-2020.

WHAT CHANGES DURING A BEAR MARKET?

1. Price level: Decreases
2. Returns: Become negative on average
3. Volatility: Often increases
4. Correlations: Increase (flight to safety)
5. Volume: Often increases

Each of these could define a "regime change." Which should we use?

FUNDAMENTAL QUESTION:

    What aspect of market behavior do we CARE about for our use case?

For TREND regime detection (BULL/BEAR/SIDEWAYS), we care about DIRECTION.
But direction is hard to capture in a single signal.


3.3 COMMON SIGNALS AND WHAT THEY DETECT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SIGNAL 1: RAW RETURNS

   r_t = (P_t - P_{t-1}) / P_{t-1}

What PELT detects: Changes in return DISTRIBUTION (mean AND variance).

Problem: Returns are noisy. Individual returns fluctuate wildly even
within a stable regime. PELT may detect noise as changepoints.

   Bull market returns: [-1%, +2%, -0.5%, +1.5%, -0.8%, +2.1%, ...]
   Bear market returns: [+1%, -2%, +0.5%, -1.5%, +0.8%, -2.1%, ...]

The overlap is huge! Hard to distinguish without smoothing.


SIGNAL 2: ROLLING MEAN RETURN

   μ_t = (1/w) Σ_{i=0}^{w-1} r_{t-i}

What PELT detects: Changes in the SMOOTHED average return.

Better for trend detection because smoothing reduces noise.

Problem: Lag. A 20-day rolling mean takes 20 days to fully reflect
a regime change.

   Regime change at t=50
   Rolling mean (w=20) doesn't fully adjust until t=70


SIGNAL 3: ROLLING VOLATILITY

   σ_t = std(r_{t-w+1:t})

What PELT detects: Changes in VOLATILITY level.

This detects volatility regimes (LOW_VOL vs HIGH_VOL), NOT trend regimes.

Problem for trend detection: A V-shaped crash-and-recovery has HIGH
volatility throughout. PELT sees one volatility regime, not two
directional regimes.


SIGNAL 4: CUMULATIVE RETURNS

   C_t = Σ_{i=1}^{t} r_i  (or C_t = P_t / P_0 - 1)

What PELT detects: Changes in the SLOPE of cumulative returns.

Good for trend detection because:
   - Bull market: C_t increases → positive slope
   - Bear market: C_t decreases → negative slope
   - Sideways: C_t flat → zero slope

PELT with RBF kernel on cumulative returns detects where the slope changes.


SIGNAL 5: PRICE RELATIVE TO MOVING AVERAGE

   D_t = P_t / SMA_t - 1

What PELT detects: Changes in price POSITION relative to trend.

   D_t > 0: Price above SMA (bullish)
   D_t < 0: Price below SMA (bearish)
   D_t ≈ 0: Price near SMA (neutral)


SIGNAL 6: MULTIVARIATE SIGNALS

   X_t = [r_t, σ_t]  (returns and volatility)

What PELT detects: Changes in EITHER returns OR volatility.

This is what our implementation uses. Problem: It conflates two
different types of regime changes.


3.4 THE V-SHAPE PROBLEM REVISITED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Recall the ICICI Bank example from our discussion:

   Price: 300 → 250 → 350  (drop then recovery)

What different signals show:

SIGNAL: [log_returns, volatility]

   During drop:     returns negative, volatility HIGH
   During recovery: returns positive, volatility HIGH

   PELT sees: Volatility is HIGH throughout → no changepoint at bottom

SIGNAL: cumulative_returns

   During drop:     cumulative return decreasing
   During recovery: cumulative return increasing

   PELT sees: Slope changes from negative to positive → changepoint at bottom!

SIGNAL: price_vs_SMA

   During drop:     price < SMA (negative)
   During recovery: price > SMA (positive, eventually)

   PELT sees: Sign change → changepoint (with some lag)

LESSON:

   The V-bottom is invisible to [returns, volatility] because volatility
   doesn't change at the bottom. It's visible to cumulative returns
   because the DIRECTION changes.


3.5 DESIGNING SIGNALS FOR TREND REGIMES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Our goal: Detect BULL/BEAR/SIDEWAYS based on price DIRECTION.

REQUIREMENTS FOR A GOOD TREND SIGNAL:

1. Captures DIRECTION (positive vs negative movement)
2. Smoothed enough to avoid noise-induced changepoints
3. Sensitive enough to detect real regime changes
4. Minimal lag (responds quickly to actual changes)

PROPOSED SIGNAL FOR TREND DETECTION:

   X_t = [rolling_return_t, momentum_t]

Where:
   rolling_return_t = (P_t - P_{t-w}) / P_{t-w}  (w-period return)
   momentum_t = P_t / SMA_t - 1                  (price vs moving average)

Why this works:

   BULL MARKET:  rolling_return > 0, momentum > 0
   BEAR MARKET:  rolling_return < 0, momentum < 0
   TRANSITION:   One changes sign before the other

PELT will detect when BOTH signals shift together (high confidence) or
when one shifts significantly (possible transition).


3.6 SIGNAL NORMALIZATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Before feeding signals to PELT, normalization is important.

WHY NORMALIZE?

Different signals have different scales:
   - Returns: typically ±5%
   - Volatility: typically 0.5% - 5%
   - Price/SMA: typically ±20%

If unnormalized, PELT weights high-variance signals more heavily.

NORMALIZATION METHODS:

1. Z-SCORE NORMALIZATION

   X_normalized = (X - mean(X)) / std(X)

   Centers at 0, scales to unit variance.
   Problem: Requires knowing mean/std in advance (look-ahead).

2. ROLLING Z-SCORE

   X_normalized_t = (X_t - rolling_mean_t) / rolling_std_t

   No look-ahead, but adapts slowly.

3. ROBUST SCALING

   X_normalized = (X - median(X)) / IQR(X)

   Uses median and interquartile range. Robust to outliers.

4. MIN-MAX SCALING

   X_normalized = (X - min(X)) / (max(X) - min(X))

   Scales to [0, 1]. Requires knowing min/max.

OUR APPROACH:

We use z-score normalization computed on the full series. This is
acceptable for BACKTESTING (we have all data) but would need to be
replaced with rolling normalization for live trading.


3.7 MULTIVARIATE SIGNAL CONSIDERATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When using multiple signals X_t = [x1_t, x2_t, ...], additional
considerations arise.

CORRELATION BETWEEN SIGNALS:

If x1 and x2 are highly correlated, they provide redundant information.
PELT effectively weights the correlated direction more heavily.

EXAMPLE 3.1 (Correlated Signals)

   x1 = rolling_return
   x2 = momentum

   These are correlated (both measure direction). A bull market has
   both x1 > 0 and x2 > 0.

   Not necessarily bad - correlation means they agree on regime.

EXAMPLE 3.2 (Uncorrelated Signals)

   x1 = rolling_return  (direction)
   x2 = rolling_volatility  (magnitude)

   These are less correlated. Bull market can have low or high volatility.

   Risk: PELT finds changepoints when EITHER changes. This conflates
   directional and volatility regimes.

DIMENSIONALITY:

More signals = more complex patterns to detect.
   - 2 signals: PELT finds changes in 2D distribution
   - 10 signals: PELT finds changes in 10D distribution

Too many signals can lead to:
   - Overfitting (finding spurious changepoints)
   - Slower computation
   - Harder interpretation

RECOMMENDATION: Use 2-3 well-chosen signals, not many correlated ones.


3.8 KERNEL CHOICE IN PELT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PELT uses a COST FUNCTION (kernel) to measure segment homogeneity.
The kernel choice affects what kind of changes are detected.

LINEAR KERNEL (L2 COST):

   C(X_{a:b}) = Σ ||X_t - X̄||²

Detects changes in MEAN.

   Before: X_t ~ N([1,1], I)
   After:  X_t ~ N([3,3], I)
   → Detected!

   Before: X_t ~ N([0,0], I)
   After:  X_t ~ N([0,0], 4I)  (same mean, different variance)
   → NOT detected!

RBF KERNEL:

   C(X_{a:b}) = n - (1/n²) Σ_{i,j} exp(-γ||X_i - X_j||²)

Detects changes in GENERAL DISTRIBUTION SHAPE.

   Can detect changes in mean, variance, skewness, or any other
   distributional property.

   More flexible but harder to interpret.

OUR CHOICE:

We use RBF kernel because regime changes may involve multiple
distributional properties (mean, variance, etc.).


3.9 PRACTICAL SIGNAL DESIGN WORKFLOW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

STEP 1: Define what "regime" means for your use case.

   - Trend regime (BULL/BEAR/SIDEWAYS)? → need directional signals
   - Volatility regime? → need volatility signals
   - Both? → need both types

STEP 2: List candidate signals.

   For trend regimes:
   - Rolling returns (various windows)
   - Momentum (price vs SMA)
   - Cumulative returns
   - SMA slope

STEP 3: Visualize signals alongside known regime changes.

   Plot signals during a known bear market (e.g., Q1 2020).
   Do they clearly indicate the regime?

STEP 4: Test PELT with different signals.

   Run PELT on each candidate signal.
   Compare detected changepoints to known regime changes.

STEP 5: Select signals that detect what you care about.

   If goal is trend detection, choose signals that find trend changes.
   Discard signals that find volatility changes instead.

STEP 6: Combine signals if needed.

   Multivariate PELT can use 2-3 complementary signals.
   Ensure signals aren't too correlated or too independent.


3.10 WHY OUR ORIGINAL SIGNAL CHOICE WAS SUBOPTIMAL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Our implementation uses:

   X_t = [log_returns_t, rolling_volatility_t]

ANALYSIS:

1. log_returns_t:
   - Very noisy (daily returns fluctuate wildly)
   - Contains directional information but obscured by noise
   - PELT finds changes in return distribution (mean AND variance)

2. rolling_volatility_t:
   - Smooth, stable signal
   - Contains NO directional information
   - PELT finds volatility regime changes

COMBINED EFFECT:

The smooth volatility signal dominates. PELT primarily finds
VOLATILITY changepoints, not TREND changepoints.

This is why the V-bottom wasn't detected: volatility was high
throughout both the drop and recovery.

BETTER ALTERNATIVE:

   X_t = [rolling_return_t, price_vs_sma_t]

Both signals contain directional information. PELT will find
changes in DIRECTION.


3.11 CHAPTER SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

KEY CONCEPTS:

1. The SIGNAL determines what PELT detects. Algorithm is agnostic to
   financial meaning - it only sees numbers.

2. For TREND regime detection, signals must capture DIRECTION:
   - Rolling returns (smoothed)
   - Price vs moving average
   - Cumulative returns

3. VOLATILITY-based signals detect volatility regimes, NOT trend regimes.
   This causes the V-shape problem.

4. NORMALIZATION ensures signals contribute equally.

5. MULTIVARIATE signals:
   - Choose complementary signals (not redundant)
   - 2-3 signals is usually sufficient
   - Too many → overfitting

6. RBF kernel detects general distributional changes.
   Linear kernel only detects mean changes.

CRITICAL LESSON:

   If you want to detect trend changes, use trend-based signals.
   Our original [returns, volatility] signal was suboptimal for
   trend regime detection.


================================================================================
                                  CHAPTER 4
                    CLUSTERING AND REGIME CLASSIFICATION
================================================================================

4.1 INTRODUCTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

After PELT segments the time series, we have boundaries but not labels.

   Segment 1: bars 1-100    → ???
   Segment 2: bars 101-250  → ???
   Segment 3: bars 251-400  → ???

We need to CLASSIFY each segment as BULL, BEAR, or SIDEWAYS.

This chapter covers clustering algorithms for regime classification.


4.2 WHY CLUSTERING?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

APPROACH 1: RULE-BASED CLASSIFICATION

   if slope > 0 and mean_return > 0:
       regime = BULL
   elif slope < 0 and mean_return < 0:
       regime = BEAR
   else:
       regime = SIDEWAYS

Problem: Requires choosing thresholds manually.
   - What if slope = 0.01? Is that BULL or SIDEWAYS?
   - Thresholds that work for SPY may fail for Bitcoin.

APPROACH 2: DATA-DRIVEN CLUSTERING

Let the algorithm learn regime boundaries from data.

   - No manual thresholds
   - Adapts to different assets/timeframes
   - Statistically principled

This is why we use Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM).


4.3 K-MEANS CLUSTERING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Before GMM, let's understand K-Means as a baseline.

ALGORITHM:

   K-Means(X, K):
       Initialize K centroids μ₁, ..., μ_K randomly
       Repeat until convergence:
           # Assignment step
           For each point x_i:
               Assign x_i to closest centroid: c_i = argmin_k ||x_i - μ_k||

           # Update step
           For each cluster k:
               μ_k = mean of all points assigned to cluster k

       Return cluster assignments

EXAMPLE 4.1 (K-Means with K=2)

   Data: [(1,1), (1.5, 0.5), (2, 1), (5, 5), (5.5, 4.5), (6, 5)]

   Initialize: μ₁ = (1, 1), μ₂ = (6, 5)

   Iteration 1:
       Point (1,1): closer to μ₁ → cluster 1
       Point (1.5, 0.5): closer to μ₁ → cluster 1
       Point (2, 1): closer to μ₁ → cluster 1
       Point (5, 5): closer to μ₂ → cluster 2
       Point (5.5, 4.5): closer to μ₂ → cluster 2
       Point (6, 5): closer to μ₂ → cluster 2

       New μ₁ = (1.5, 0.83), New μ₂ = (5.5, 4.83)

   Converged (assignments don't change).

LIMITATIONS OF K-MEANS:

1. Hard assignments: Each point belongs to exactly one cluster.
   No uncertainty quantification.

2. Spherical clusters: Assumes clusters are roughly spherical.
   Fails for elongated or non-convex clusters.

3. Equal cluster sizes: Tends to create equal-sized clusters
   even when natural clusters have different sizes.


4.4 GAUSSIAN MIXTURE MODELS (GMM)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

GMM addresses K-Means limitations by modeling clusters as probability
distributions.

GENERATIVE MODEL:

   For each data point x_i:
       1. Choose cluster k with probability π_k (mixing weight)
       2. Draw x_i from Gaussian: x_i ~ N(μ_k, Σ_k)

MATHEMATICAL FORMULATION:

   P(x) = Σ_{k=1}^{K} π_k · N(x | μ_k, Σ_k)

Where:
   - π_k = probability of cluster k (mixing weights, Σπ_k = 1)
   - μ_k = mean of cluster k
   - Σ_k = covariance matrix of cluster k
   - N(x | μ, Σ) = Gaussian density

KEY DIFFERENCE FROM K-MEANS:

K-Means: Point x belongs to cluster k with probability 0 or 1.
GMM: Point x belongs to cluster k with probability P(k|x) ∈ [0, 1].

EXAMPLE 4.2 (GMM Soft Assignments)

   Point x is equidistant from two cluster centers.

   K-Means: Arbitrarily assigns to one cluster.
   GMM: P(cluster 1 | x) = 0.5, P(cluster 2 | x) = 0.5

   This uncertainty is informative! The point is genuinely ambiguous.


4.5 EXPECTATION-MAXIMIZATION (EM) ALGORITHM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

GMM parameters (π, μ, Σ) are learned via the EM algorithm.

GOAL: Maximize log-likelihood

   L(θ) = Σ_i log P(x_i | θ) = Σ_i log [Σ_k π_k · N(x_i | μ_k, Σ_k)]

PROBLEM: This is hard to optimize directly because of the sum inside log.

SOLUTION: EM algorithm iterates between two steps.

E-STEP (Expectation):

   Compute responsibility of cluster k for point i:

   γ_{ik} = P(z_i = k | x_i) = [π_k · N(x_i | μ_k, Σ_k)] / [Σ_j π_j · N(x_i | μ_j, Σ_j)]

   Interpretation: γ_{ik} is probability that point i came from cluster k.

M-STEP (Maximization):

   Update parameters using responsibilities:

   N_k = Σ_i γ_{ik}                           (effective cluster size)
   π_k = N_k / N                              (mixing weight)
   μ_k = (1/N_k) Σ_i γ_{ik} · x_i            (weighted mean)
   Σ_k = (1/N_k) Σ_i γ_{ik} · (x_i - μ_k)(x_i - μ_k)ᵀ  (weighted covariance)

ALGORITHM:

   EM(X, K):
       Initialize parameters (π, μ, Σ) randomly
       Repeat until convergence:
           # E-step
           For each point i and cluster k:
               γ_{ik} = P(k | x_i)  using current parameters

           # M-step
           Update π, μ, Σ using γ

       Return parameters and responsibilities

CONVERGENCE:

EM is guaranteed to increase (or maintain) the likelihood at each step.
However, it may converge to a LOCAL maximum, not global.

Solution: Run EM multiple times with different initializations,
keep the result with highest likelihood.


4.6 COVARIANCE TYPES IN GMM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The covariance matrix Σ_k determines the shape of cluster k.

FULL COVARIANCE:

   Σ_k is a full d×d positive definite matrix.
   Cluster can be any ellipsoid shape and orientation.

   Parameters per cluster: d + d(d+1)/2 = d(d+3)/2
   For d=4: 14 parameters per cluster

   Most flexible but most parameters → risk of overfitting.

TIED COVARIANCE:

   All clusters share the same covariance: Σ_k = Σ for all k.
   Clusters have same shape but different centers.

   Parameters: K·d + d(d+1)/2
   Fewer parameters, less flexible.

DIAGONAL COVARIANCE:

   Σ_k is diagonal (features are independent given cluster).
   Clusters are axis-aligned ellipsoids.

   Parameters per cluster: d + d = 2d
   For d=4: 8 parameters per cluster

SPHERICAL COVARIANCE:

   Σ_k = σ_k² · I (clusters are spheres).
   Parameters per cluster: d + 1
   For d=4: 5 parameters per cluster

   Most restrictive, equivalent to K-Means with soft assignments.

OUR CHOICE:

We use FULL covariance because regime clusters may have complex shapes.
For example, SIDEWAYS regime might have high variance in volatility
but low variance in return direction.


4.7 SELECTING NUMBER OF CLUSTERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

How many regimes (clusters) should we use?

METHOD 1: DOMAIN KNOWLEDGE

   For trend regimes: K=3 (BULL, BEAR, SIDEWAYS) is natural.
   This is our choice.

METHOD 2: BIC (BAYESIAN INFORMATION CRITERION)

   BIC = -2·log(L) + p·log(N)

   Where:
   - L = maximized likelihood
   - p = number of parameters
   - N = number of data points

   Lower BIC is better. Penalizes complexity.

   TRY K = 2, 3, 4, 5
   Choose K with lowest BIC.

METHOD 3: SILHOUETTE SCORE

   For each point i:
       a(i) = average distance to points in same cluster
       b(i) = average distance to points in nearest other cluster
       s(i) = (b(i) - a(i)) / max(a(i), b(i))

   Silhouette score = mean of s(i) over all points.
   Higher is better (max = 1).

METHOD 4: ELBOW METHOD

   Plot likelihood vs K.
   Find "elbow" where adding clusters doesn't help much.

PRACTICAL CONSIDERATION:

For regime detection, K=3 is standard (BULL/BEAR/SIDEWAYS).
Using more clusters creates sub-regimes that may be harder to interpret.


4.8 SEGMENT-LEVEL VS BAR-LEVEL CLUSTERING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A key design choice: what do we cluster?

OPTION A: CLUSTER SEGMENTS

   Compute features for each segment, then cluster segments.

   Features per segment: [mean_slope, mean_R², mean_return, mean_vol]

   Number of points: Number of segments (often 5-20)

   Problem: Very few data points! GMM can't fit reliably.

OPTION B: CLUSTER BARS

   Compute features for each bar, then cluster bars.

   Features per bar: [slope, R², return, vol]

   Number of points: Number of bars (often 500-2000)

   Then: Assign segment label by majority vote of its bars.

   Advantage: Many more data points for robust clustering.

OUR CHOICE:

We cluster BARS, not segments. This ensures we have enough data points
for reliable GMM fitting regardless of how many segments PELT finds.

MAJORITY VOTE:

   For segment with bars [b1, b2, ..., bn]:
       count = [count of bars in cluster 0, 1, 2]
       segment_cluster = argmax(count)

   Example:
       Segment has 100 bars.
       Bar clusters: 70 in cluster 1, 25 in cluster 2, 5 in cluster 0
       Segment assigned to cluster 1.


4.9 FROM CLUSTERS TO REGIME LABELS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

GMM gives us cluster assignments (0, 1, 2), not regime labels (BULL, BEAR, SIDEWAYS).

How do we map cluster IDs to meaningful labels?

APPROACH: INTERPRET CLUSTER CENTERS

Each GMM cluster has a center μ_k = [slope_k, r2_k, return_k, vol_k].

INTERPRETATION RULES:

   If slope > 0 AND mean_return > 0:
       → BULL (price going up)

   If slope < 0 AND mean_return < 0:
       → BEAR (price going down)

   If slope ≈ 0 OR signals conflict OR R² very low:
       → SIDEWAYS (no clear trend)

EXAMPLE 4.3 (Cluster Interpretation)

   Cluster 0 center: [+0.05, 0.8, +0.1%, 1.0%]
       → Positive slope, high R², positive return
       → BULL

   Cluster 1 center: [-0.08, 0.7, -0.15%, 1.5%]
       → Negative slope, high R², negative return
       → BEAR

   Cluster 2 center: [+0.01, 0.2, -0.02%, 2.0%]
       → Slope near zero, low R², conflicting return
       → SIDEWAYS

HANDLING EDGE CASES:

What if two clusters both map to BULL?
   → Merge them, or treat one as "strong bull" vs "weak bull"

What if no cluster maps to BEAR?
   → The data may not contain a bear market period
   → BEAR regime will have 0% of time


4.10 PRACTICAL ISSUES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ISSUE 1: SINGULAR COVARIANCE

If a cluster has very few points or points are nearly identical,
the covariance matrix becomes singular (non-invertible).

Solution: Add regularization.

   Σ_k = Σ_k + ε·I

Our implementation uses reg_covar=1e-5 in sklearn GMM.

ISSUE 2: NUMERICAL OVERFLOW

With high-dimensional features or extreme values, Gaussian densities
can overflow (become 0 or infinity).

Solution: Clip extreme values before clustering.

   X_clipped = np.clip(X, -10, 10)  # after normalization

ISSUE 3: RANDOM INITIALIZATION

GMM with random initialization may converge to different solutions.

Solution: Use multiple initializations (n_init=10), keep best.

ISSUE 4: EMPTY CLUSTERS

Sometimes a cluster ends up with no points.

Solution: sklearn handles this automatically by reinitializing.


4.11 CHAPTER SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

KEY CONCEPTS:

1. After PELT segments the data, we need to CLASSIFY segments into regimes.

2. GMM provides data-driven clustering without manual thresholds:
   - Learns regime characteristics from data
   - Provides soft assignments (uncertainty)
   - Flexible cluster shapes (full covariance)

3. EM algorithm learns GMM parameters:
   - E-step: Compute point responsibilities
   - M-step: Update parameters
   - Converges to (local) maximum likelihood

4. We cluster BARS, not segments, for statistical reliability.
   Then assign segment labels by majority vote.

5. Cluster centers are interpreted using feature signs:
   - Positive slope + positive return → BULL
   - Negative slope + negative return → BEAR
   - Conflicting or weak signals → SIDEWAYS

6. Practical issues (singularity, overflow, initialization) require
   careful handling: regularization, clipping, multiple inits.

                                  CHAPTER 5
                 HIDDEN MARKOV MODELS FOR REGIME DETECTION
================================================================================

5.1 INTRODUCTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) are a powerful alternative to PELT+GMM for
regime detection. This chapter explains HMM theory and compares approaches.


5.2 HMM INTUITION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ANALOGY: THE WEATHER AND UMBRELLA PROBLEM

Imagine you're locked in a basement with no windows. Each day, someone
enters carrying either an umbrella or no umbrella. From observing
umbrellas, can you infer the weather sequence?

   Weather (hidden):  [Sunny, Sunny, Rainy, Rainy, Sunny, ...]
   Umbrella (observed): [No, No, Yes, Yes, No, ...]

The weather is the HIDDEN STATE. The umbrella is the OBSERVATION.
Weather today depends on weather yesterday (Markov property).

MARKET ANALOGY:

   Regime (hidden):   [BULL, BULL, BEAR, BEAR, SIDEWAYS, ...]
   Returns (observed): [+1%, +0.5%, -2%, -1.5%, +0.1%, ...]

We observe returns. We want to infer the hidden regime sequence.


5.3 HMM FORMAL DEFINITION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

COMPONENTS:

1. HIDDEN STATES: S = {s_1, s_2, ..., s_K}
   For regime detection: S = {BULL, BEAR, SIDEWAYS}

2. OBSERVATIONS: {X_t}_{t=1}^{T}
   For markets: daily returns, prices, or features

3. INITIAL DISTRIBUTION: π_k = P(S_1 = s_k)
   Probability of starting in each state.

4. TRANSITION MATRIX: A_{jk} = P(S_t = s_k | S_{t-1} = s_j)
   Probability of moving from state j to state k.

   Example (3-state):
                   BULL  BEAR  SIDE
   From BULL:     [0.95  0.03  0.02]
   From BEAR:     [0.05  0.90  0.05]
   From SIDEWAYS: [0.10  0.10  0.80]

   Interpretation: 95% chance of staying in BULL, 3% of transitioning to BEAR.

5. EMISSION DISTRIBUTION: B_k(x) = P(X_t = x | S_t = s_k)
   Probability of observing x when in state k.

   Often Gaussian: X_t | S_t = k ~ N(μ_k, Σ_k)

MARKOV PROPERTY:

   P(S_t | S_1, ..., S_{t-1}) = P(S_t | S_{t-1})

The future state depends only on the current state, not the full history.


5.4 THE THREE FUNDAMENTAL HMM PROBLEMS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM 1: EVALUATION (LIKELIHOOD)

   Given: Model parameters λ = (π, A, B), observation sequence X_{1:T}
   Find: P(X_{1:T} | λ)

   "How likely is this observation sequence under the model?"

PROBLEM 2: DECODING (STATE INFERENCE)

   Given: Model parameters λ, observation sequence X_{1:T}
   Find: Most likely state sequence S_{1:T}*

   "What regime was the market in at each time?"

   This is what we want for regime detection!

PROBLEM 3: LEARNING (PARAMETER ESTIMATION)

   Given: Observation sequence X_{1:T}
   Find: Model parameters λ* that maximize P(X_{1:T} | λ)

   "Learn the regime characteristics from data."


5.5 THE FORWARD ALGORITHM (PROBLEM 1)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Computes P(X_{1:T} | λ) efficiently.

NAIVE APPROACH:

   Sum over all possible state sequences:
   P(X) = Σ_{S} P(X, S) = Σ_{S} P(X|S) P(S)

   Problem: K^T possible sequences → exponential complexity!

FORWARD ALGORITHM:

Define forward variable:
   α_t(k) = P(X_{1:t}, S_t = k | λ)
   "Probability of observations up to t AND being in state k at time t"

RECURSION:

   Initialization:
       α_1(k) = π_k · B_k(X_1)

   Induction:
       α_t(k) = [Σ_j α_{t-1}(j) · A_{jk}] · B_k(X_t)

   Termination:
       P(X_{1:T} | λ) = Σ_k α_T(k)

COMPLEXITY: O(K²T) - polynomial, not exponential!

INTUITION:

At each time t, we only track K values (one per state), not all
possible paths. The Markov property allows this compression.


5.6 THE BACKWARD ALGORITHM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Computes "future" probabilities, used for learning.

Define backward variable:
   β_t(k) = P(X_{t+1:T} | S_t = k, λ)
   "Probability of future observations GIVEN being in state k at time t"

RECURSION:

   Initialization:
       β_T(k) = 1 for all k

   Induction (going backwards):
       β_t(k) = Σ_j A_{kj} · B_j(X_{t+1}) · β_{t+1}(j)

COMBINING FORWARD AND BACKWARD:

   P(S_t = k | X_{1:T}) ∝ α_t(k) · β_t(k)

This gives the POSTERIOR probability of being in state k at time t,
given ALL observations (past AND future).


5.7 THE VITERBI ALGORITHM (PROBLEM 2)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Finds the most likely state sequence.

NAIVE APPROACH:

   Check all K^T sequences, find one with highest P(S | X).
   Exponential complexity!

VITERBI ALGORITHM (Dynamic Programming):

Define:
   δ_t(k) = max_{S_{1:t-1}} P(S_{1:t-1}, S_t = k, X_{1:t})
   "Probability of best path ending in state k at time t"

RECURSION:

   Initialization:
       δ_1(k) = π_k · B_k(X_1)
       ψ_1(k) = 0  (no predecessor)

   Induction:
       δ_t(k) = max_j [δ_{t-1}(j) · A_{jk}] · B_k(X_t)
       ψ_t(k) = argmax_j [δ_{t-1}(j) · A_{jk}]

   Termination:
       S_T* = argmax_k δ_T(k)

   Backtracking:
       S_t* = ψ_{t+1}(S_{t+1}*)

RESULT: Most likely state sequence S_{1:T}*.

COMPLEXITY: O(K²T)


5.8 THE BAUM-WELCH ALGORITHM (PROBLEM 3)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Learns HMM parameters from data using EM.

E-STEP: Compute expected state occupancies and transitions.

Define:
   γ_t(k) = P(S_t = k | X_{1:T}, λ)
          = α_t(k) · β_t(k) / P(X)

   ξ_t(j,k) = P(S_t = j, S_{t+1} = k | X_{1:T}, λ)
            = α_t(j) · A_{jk} · B_k(X_{t+1}) · β_{t+1}(k) / P(X)

M-STEP: Update parameters.

   π_k = γ_1(k)

   A_{jk} = Σ_t ξ_t(j,k) / Σ_t γ_t(j)

   For Gaussian emissions:
       μ_k = Σ_t γ_t(k) · X_t / Σ_t γ_t(k)
       Σ_k = Σ_t γ_t(k) · (X_t - μ_k)(X_t - μ_k)ᵀ / Σ_t γ_t(k)

ITERATION: Repeat E and M steps until convergence.


5.9 HMM VS PELT+GMM COMPARISON
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

                        HMM                     PELT+GMM
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Regime structure        Markov transitions      Independent segments

Temporal modeling       Explicit (transition    Implicit (through
                        probabilities)          changepoint detection)

Output                  Probabilistic states    Hard segment labels
                        at each time

Changepoint detection   Implicit (transition    Explicit (PELT finds
                        probability spikes)     boundaries)

Computational cost      O(K²T) per EM iter      O(T) PELT + O(NK²) GMM

Look-ahead bias         Standard Viterbi uses   PELT is non-causal
                        future observations     (uses full series)

Number of regimes       Must specify K          Must specify K

Interpretability        Moderate (need to       Good (segments have
                        examine posteriors)     clear features)

Regime persistence      Built-in (transition    Enforced by min
                        matrix encourages       segment length
                        staying in state)


5.10 LOOK-AHEAD BIAS IN HMM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

IMPORTANT WARNING FOR BACKTESTING:

Standard HMM algorithms (Viterbi, Forward-Backward) use FUTURE observations
to infer PAST states.

EXAMPLE:

   At time t=100, Viterbi uses observations X_{1:200} to decide S_{100}.
   This means S_{100} depends on information not available at t=100.

FOR BACKTESTING: This is acceptable because we're doing POST-HOC analysis.
We want the best estimate of regimes given all data.

FOR LIVE TRADING: Cannot use standard HMM. Must use FILTERING:
   P(S_t | X_{1:t})  not  P(S_t | X_{1:T})

This gives the online estimate using only past observations.


5.11 WHEN TO USE HMM VS PELT+GMM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

USE HMM WHEN:

1. Regime persistence matters: HMM naturally models tendency to stay
   in same regime (high diagonal in transition matrix).

2. You want probabilistic output: HMM gives P(regime) at each time,
   not hard labels.

3. Regime transitions are gradual: HMM can model soft transitions.

USE PELT+GMM WHEN:

1. Interpretability is key: Explicit segments with features are
   easier to understand and explain.

2. Computational simplicity: PELT is O(T), faster than EM iterations.

3. Regime changes are abrupt: PELT is designed for sharp changepoints.

4. Minimum segment length is important: PELT enforces this directly.

OUR CHOICE:

We use PELT+GMM because:
- Explicit segment boundaries are more interpretable
- Easier to explain regime features to users
- Computational simplicity
- Our regimes (BULL/BEAR/SIDEWAYS) have reasonably sharp transitions


5.12 CHAPTER SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

KEY CONCEPTS:

1. HMM models regimes as hidden states with Markov transitions.

2. Three fundamental problems:
   - Evaluation: Forward algorithm
   - Decoding: Viterbi algorithm
   - Learning: Baum-Welch (EM)

3. HMM pros:
   - Principled probabilistic framework
   - Models regime persistence naturally
   - Soft state assignments

4. HMM cons:
   - Look-ahead bias in standard algorithms
   - Less interpretable than explicit segments
   - More complex implementation

5. For backtesting analysis, both HMM and PELT+GMM are valid.
   We chose PELT+GMM for interpretability.


================================================================================
                                  CHAPTER 6
                 FEATURE ENGINEERING FOR REGIME CLASSIFICATION
================================================================================

6.1 INTRODUCTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

After PELT segments the data, we need FEATURES to classify each segment.
Good features capture the essence of regime differences. Bad features
lead to misclassification.

This chapter details the feature engineering for regime detection.


6.2 WHAT MAKES A GOOD FEATURE?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CRITERIA FOR REGIME FEATURES:

1. DISCRIMINATIVE: Different regimes should have different feature values.

   Good: Slope (BULL ≈ +0.1, BEAR ≈ -0.1, SIDEWAYS ≈ 0)
   Bad: Volume (similar across regimes)

2. ROBUST: Not overly sensitive to noise or outliers.

   Good: R² (bounded 0-1, resistant to outliers)
   Bad: Max daily return (single outlier dominates)

3. INTERPRETABLE: Can explain what feature captures.

   Good: "Average daily return percentage"
   Bad: "Third principal component of wavelet coefficients"

4. COMPUTABLE: Can calculate from available data.

   Good: Derived from OHLCV
   Bad: Requires sentiment data we don't have


6.3 OUR FEATURE SET
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We use four features per bar:

FEATURE 1: SLOPE (from OLS regression)

   For segment [a, b], fit: P_t = α + β·t + ε

   slope = β / mean(P)  (normalized by price level)

   Interpretation:
   - Positive slope → prices trending up
   - Negative slope → prices trending down
   - Near-zero slope → flat

   Units: Percent change per bar


FEATURE 2: R² (coefficient of determination)

   R² = 1 - SS_res / SS_tot
      = 1 - Σ(P_t - P̂_t)² / Σ(P_t - P̄)²

   Interpretation:
   - R² near 1 → strong linear trend
   - R² near 0 → no linear trend (noisy or curved)

   Why it matters:
   - High slope + high R² = confident trend
   - High slope + low R² = unreliable trend
   - Low slope + low R² = sideways/choppy

   Range: [0, 1]


FEATURE 3: MEAN RETURN

   mean_return = (1/n) Σ r_t  where r_t = (P_t - P_{t-1}) / P_{t-1}

   Interpretation:
   - Positive mean → gaining on average
   - Negative mean → losing on average

   Difference from slope:
   - Slope: Overall direction from start to end
   - Mean return: Average of daily moves

   These usually agree, but can differ:
   - V-shape: Slope ≈ 0, but mean return depends on timing


FEATURE 4: STANDARD DEVIATION OF RETURNS

   std_return = std(r_t)

   Interpretation:
   - High std → volatile, large daily swings
   - Low std → calm, small daily moves

   Useful for distinguishing:
   - SIDEWAYS + low std = quiet consolidation
   - SIDEWAYS + high std = choppy, whipsaw-prone


6.4 SEGMENT-WIDE VS LOCAL FEATURES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SEGMENT-WIDE FEATURES:

Computed once for the entire segment.

   Example: OLS slope fitted to all bars in segment.

   Pros: Captures overall segment character
   Cons: Misses variation within segment

LOCAL FEATURES:

Computed for each bar using a rolling window.

   Example: 10-bar rolling mean return.

   Pros: Captures local variation
   Cons: May be noisy; which window size?

OUR APPROACH:

We compute BOTH:
   - Slope and R²: Segment-wide (stable trend direction)
   - Mean return and std: Local rolling window (captures variation)

Then assign segment-wide values to all bars in segment (for clustering).


6.5 OLS REGRESSION FOR TREND ESTIMATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SETUP:

Given segment prices P_{t_1}, P_{t_2}, ..., P_{t_n}, fit:

   P_t = α + β·(t - t_1) + ε

where t - t_1 is bars since segment start.

SOLUTION (via calculus):

   β = Cov(t, P) / Var(t) = [Σ(t - t̄)(P - P̄)] / [Σ(t - t̄)²]
   α = P̄ - β·t̄

For equally spaced t = 0, 1, ..., n-1:
   t̄ = (n-1)/2
   Σ(t - t̄)² = n(n²-1)/12

EXAMPLE 6.1

   Prices: [100, 102, 101, 104, 106]
   Time:   [0, 1, 2, 3, 4]

   n = 5, t̄ = 2, P̄ = 102.6

   β = [(0-2)(100-102.6) + (1-2)(102-102.6) + ... + (4-2)(106-102.6)]
       / [(0-2)² + (1-2)² + ... + (4-2)²]

     = [(-2)(-2.6) + (-1)(-0.6) + (0)(-1.6) + (1)(1.4) + (2)(3.4)]
       / [4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4]

     = [5.2 + 0.6 + 0 + 1.4 + 6.8] / 10
     = 14 / 10 = 1.4

   Normalized slope: β / P̄ = 1.4 / 102.6 = 0.0136 = 1.36% per bar


R² CALCULATION:

   P̂_t = α + β·t  (fitted values)

   SS_tot = Σ(P_t - P̄)² = (-2.6)² + (-0.6)² + (-1.6)² + (1.4)² + (3.4)²
          = 6.76 + 0.36 + 2.56 + 1.96 + 11.56 = 23.2

   SS_res = Σ(P_t - P̂_t)²

   First compute P̂:
   P̂_0 = 102.6 - 1.4(2) = 99.8
   P̂_1 = 102.6 - 1.4(1) = 101.2
   P̂_2 = 102.6 - 1.4(0) = 102.6
   P̂_3 = 102.6 + 1.4(1) = 104.0
   P̂_4 = 102.6 + 1.4(2) = 105.4

   Residuals: 100-99.8=0.2, 102-101.2=0.8, 101-102.6=-1.6, 104-104=0, 106-105.4=0.6
   SS_res = 0.04 + 0.64 + 2.56 + 0 + 0.36 = 3.6

   R² = 1 - 3.6/23.2 = 1 - 0.155 = 0.845

   Interpretation: 84.5% of price variance explained by linear trend.
   This is a reasonably strong trend.


6.6 HANDLING EDGE CASES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EDGE CASE 1: VERY SHORT SEGMENTS

   Segment with 3 bars: OLS regression is unreliable.

   Solution: Return slope = 0, R² = 0 for segments < 5 bars.
   These segments lack statistical reliability.

EDGE CASE 2: CONSTANT PRICES

   All prices identical: Var(P) = 0, R² undefined (0/0).

   Solution: Return slope = 0, R² = 1 (perfect "fit" to flat line).

EDGE CASE 3: EXTREME VALUES

   Single outlier can dominate slope and R².

   Example: [100, 101, 100, 101, 500, 101, 100]

   Solution: We don't explicitly handle this; rely on PELT to segment
   around extreme events. Could use robust regression as enhancement.

EDGE CASE 4: GAPS IN DATA

   Missing bars due to holidays, data issues.

   Solution: Our data is daily OHLCV without gaps (filled by data provider).
   If gaps existed, would need to handle time index carefully.


6.7 FEATURE NORMALIZATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Before GMM clustering, features should be normalized.

WHY NORMALIZE?

   Slope: Range roughly [-0.5, +0.5]
   R²: Range [0, 1]
   Mean return: Range roughly [-0.5%, +0.5%]
   Std return: Range roughly [0.5%, 5%]

Without normalization, GMM gives more weight to features with larger
absolute values (std_return dominates due to scale).

Z-SCORE NORMALIZATION:

   For each feature f:
       f_normalized = (f - mean(f)) / std(f)

   After normalization, all features have mean 0 and std 1.

   Important: We normalize across ALL bars, not per segment.
   This ensures consistent scaling.

CLIPPING EXTREME VALUES:

   After normalization, clip to [-10, +10] to prevent numerical issues.

   X_normalized = clip(X_normalized, -10, 10)

   Values beyond ±10 standard deviations are extreme outliers;
   capping them prevents GMM instability.


6.8 FEATURE COMBINATIONS AND INTERACTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Our four features capture different aspects:

   Slope:       Direction of trend
   R²:          Strength/reliability of trend
   Mean return: Average performance
   Std return:  Volatility

EXPECTED PATTERNS BY REGIME:

   BULL:     slope > 0, R² high, mean_return > 0, std varies
   BEAR:     slope < 0, R² high, mean_return < 0, std often high
   SIDEWAYS: slope ≈ 0, R² low, mean_return ≈ 0, std varies

INTERACTIONS:

1. Slope × R²:
   - High slope + high R² = strong confident trend
   - High slope + low R² = unreliable trend (might be noise)

2. Slope × Mean return:
   - Usually same sign (both positive or both negative)
   - If different: V-shape or complex pattern

3. R² × Std:
   - High R² + low std = clean trend
   - High R² + high std = strong trend despite volatility
   - Low R² + high std = chaos, no predictability


6.9 ALTERNATIVE FEATURES (NOT USED)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Other features we considered but didn't include:

SHARPE RATIO:
   Sharpe = mean_return / std_return

   Pro: Single metric combining return and risk.
   Con: Undefined when std = 0; can be misleading for short periods.

MAX DRAWDOWN:
   Maximum peak-to-trough decline within segment.

   Pro: Captures downside risk.
   Con: Requires more computation; path-dependent.

HURST EXPONENT:
   Measures long-range dependence / trending vs mean-reverting.

   Pro: Captures market character.
   Con: Requires long segments for reliable estimation.

AUTOCORRELATION:
   Correlation of returns with lagged returns.

   Pro: Detects momentum vs mean-reversion.
   Con: Noisy for short segments.

WHY WE KEPT IT SIMPLE:

Four features (slope, R², mean_return, std_return) provide sufficient
discrimination for BULL/BEAR/SIDEWAYS while remaining interpretable.
More features risk overfitting and reduce interpretability.


6.10 CHAPTER SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

KEY CONCEPTS:

1. Good features are DISCRIMINATIVE, ROBUST, INTERPRETABLE, and COMPUTABLE.

2. Our feature set:
   - Slope: Trend direction (OLS regression)
   - R²: Trend reliability
   - Mean return: Average daily performance
   - Std return: Volatility

3. OLS regression provides slope and R²:
   - β = Cov(t, P) / Var(t)
   - R² = 1 - SS_res / SS_tot

4. Segment-wide features (slope, R²) capture overall direction.
   Local features (mean, std) capture variation.

5. Normalization is essential before GMM clustering.
   Z-score normalization puts all features on same scale.

6. Edge cases (short segments, constant prices) require special handling.

                                  CHAPTER 7
                    PATHOLOGICAL CASES AND EDGE CONDITIONS
================================================================================

7.1 INTRODUCTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

No algorithm handles all market patterns perfectly. This chapter examines
PATHOLOGICAL CASES where regime detection fails or gives misleading results.

Understanding failure modes is crucial for proper use and interpretation.


7.2 THE V-SHAPE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PATTERN:
   Price drops sharply, then recovers sharply.

   Price:  100 → 80 → 100
   Shape:   \/

EXPECTED: Two regimes (BEAR during drop, BULL during recovery)
ACTUAL (our implementation): Often one segment labeled BULL or SIDEWAYS

WHY IT FAILS:

1. PELT with [returns, volatility] signal:
   - Volatility is HIGH throughout (both drop and recovery are volatile)
   - No changepoint detected at the bottom

2. Features for combined segment:
   - Slope ≈ 0 (starts and ends at same price)
   - R² low (not a linear trend)
   - Mean return ≈ 0 (losses and gains cancel)

3. GMM classifies as SIDEWAYS or misclassifies based on noise.

SOLUTIONS:

a) Use directional signal for PELT:
   - Cumulative returns change slope at bottom
   - Rolling SMA slope changes sign at bottom

b) Post-process: Check for internal V-shape in segments with low R².

c) Accept limitation: Note that V-shapes may be misclassified.


7.3 THE W-BOTTOM / M-TOP PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PATTERN:
   Multiple reversals create W (bottom) or M (top) shape.

   W-bottom:  100 → 80 → 90 → 80 → 100
   M-top:     100 → 120 → 110 → 120 → 100

EXPECTED: Multiple regimes capturing each leg
ACTUAL: Often one segment, misclassified

WHY IT FAILS:

Similar to V-shape but worse:
- Multiple direction changes within segment
- Overall slope near zero
- Low R² (not linear)

The pattern is too complex for our linear trend features.

SOLUTIONS:

a) Lower PELT penalty to detect more changepoints.

b) Use higher-frequency features that capture short-term reversals.

c) Accept as SIDEWAYS (which is arguably correct - choppy, no clear trend).


7.4 THE WHIPSAW PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PATTERN:
   Rapid alternation between up and down moves.

   Price: 100 → 105 → 98 → 103 → 95 → 102 → 97 → ...

EXPECTED: SIDEWAYS (no sustained direction)
ACTUAL: May be split into many tiny segments or mislabeled

WHY IT FAILS:

1. If min_segment_length is low:
   - PELT may create many 5-10 bar segments
   - Each segment too short for reliable features

2. If min_segment_length is high:
   - PELT creates one large segment
   - Features may show slight directional bias from noise
   - Could be mislabeled as weak BULL or BEAR

SOLUTIONS:

a) Set min_segment_length appropriately (we use 20 bars).

b) Require minimum segment length for confident labeling.

c) Label very short segments as UNKNOWN or low-confidence.


7.5 LOW R² WITH DIRECTIONAL DRIFT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PATTERN:
   Price trends upward but with high noise / low linearity.

   Example: Net +20% over segment but with wild swings.
   Slope > 0, Mean return > 0, BUT R² = 0.2

EXPECTED: BULL (it did go up)
ACTUAL: May be labeled SIDEWAYS (due to low R²)

THIS IS A DESIGN CHOICE:

Arguments for BULL:
- Net direction was up
- A trader would have made money holding

Arguments for SIDEWAYS:
- Path was not a clean trend
- Trend-following strategies would have been stopped out repeatedly
- Low predictability (R²)

OUR CHOICE:

We lean toward calling this SIDEWAYS because:
- For strategy analysis, path matters as much as outcome
- Trend-following strategies perform poorly in low-R² environments
- The label "BULL" implies a tradeable trend, not just net positive returns

Users should interpret SIDEWAYS as "not a clean trend" rather than
"didn't go anywhere."


7.6 CONFLICTING SIGNALS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PATTERN:
   Different features disagree on regime.

   Example: slope = +0.05 (bullish), mean_return = -0.02 (bearish)

HOW THIS HAPPENS:

   V-shape shifted in time:
   - Segment starts mid-decline, ends mid-recovery
   - OLS slope may be positive (recovery steeper than decline)
   - Mean daily return negative (more down days than up days)

OUR HANDLING:

   In assign_segment_regimes(), we check for conflicting signals:

   if (slope_bullish and mean_bearish) or (slope_bearish and mean_bullish):
       regime = SIDEWAYS

RATIONALE:

When features disagree, we lack confidence in any directional label.
SIDEWAYS is the conservative choice indicating uncertainty.


7.7 SHORT SEGMENTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PATTERN:
   PELT creates a segment with only 20-30 bars.

PROBLEMS:

1. Statistical reliability:
   - OLS on 20 points has high uncertainty
   - R² may be misleading (can be high or low by chance)

2. Actionability:
   - 20 trading days ≈ 1 month
   - May be too short to be a meaningful "regime"

3. Classification:
   - GMM trained on all bars may not represent short segment well
   - Majority vote among 20 bars may be dominated by noise

OUR HANDLING:

   - min_segment_length = 20 prevents very short segments
   - We don't have special handling for borderline (20-40 bar) segments
   - Users should interpret short segments with caution

POSSIBLE ENHANCEMENT:

   Add confidence score based on segment length:
   - confidence = min(1, segment_length / 50)
   - Display confidence alongside regime label


7.8 REGIME CHANGES WITHIN WINDOWS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PATTERN:
   True regime change occurs, but PELT doesn't detect it.
   The changepoint falls within a segment.

   True regimes: BULL | BEAR
   PELT output:  ───────────── (one segment)

WHY IT HAPPENS:

1. Penalty too high: Change wasn't "significant" enough.

2. Signal insensitive: The signal used doesn't capture this type of change.

3. Change too gradual: Not a sharp changepoint.

DETECTION:

Hard to detect automatically. Signs to look for:
- Large segment with low R²
- Large segment with features not strongly in any regime
- Large segment spanning known market events

SOLUTIONS:

a) Lower PELT penalty.

b) Use more sensitive signal.

c) Add domain knowledge (split around known events like COVID crash).

d) Post-process: If segment is very long, check for internal changepoints.


7.9 BOUNDARY EFFECTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PATTERN:
   Regime change at very start or very end of data.

   Data: [■■■■|○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○]
   True:  BEAR |     BULL

   If BEAR portion is only 30 bars, PELT may not detect the changepoint.

WHY IT HAPPENS:

   PELT needs enough data on both sides of changepoint to detect it.
   Near boundaries, one side is very short.

OUR HANDLING:

   No special handling. Users should:
   - Start data before any expected regime of interest
   - Not rely heavily on regime labels near data boundaries


7.10 ALL BARS SIMILAR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PATTERN:
   GMM clustering finds that all bars are similar.
   All bars assigned to one cluster.

WHEN THIS HAPPENS:

- Single-regime data (entire period is BULL)
- Very homogeneous market conditions
- Features don't discriminate well for this data

IMPLICATIONS:

- Regime dependency analysis becomes meaningless
- "INDEPENDENT" assessment is trivially true

OUR HANDLING:

   CV-based dependency check handles this:
   - If only one regime has trades → CV = 0 → INDEPENDENT
   - This is technically correct but uninformative

RECOMMENDATION:

   Use longer time periods that span multiple known regimes.
   Single-regime analysis tells you little about regime dependency.


7.11 NUMERICAL ISSUES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ISSUE 1: DIVISION BY ZERO

   - R² calculation when Var(P) = 0
   - Slope normalization when mean(P) = 0

   Solution: Check for zero denominators, return default values.

ISSUE 2: OVERFLOW IN GMM

   - Gaussian density for extreme values → 0 or infinity
   - Covariance matrix becomes singular

   Solution: Clip features to [-10, +10] after normalization.
   Use regularized covariance (reg_covar=1e-5).

ISSUE 3: LOG OF ZERO

   - Log returns when P_{t-1} = 0 (shouldn't happen with prices)

   Solution: Data validation should catch this.


7.12 CHAPTER SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PATHOLOGICAL CASES:

1. V-SHAPE: Volatility stays high, no changepoint at bottom.
   → Consider directional signal for PELT.

2. W/M PATTERNS: Multiple reversals, low R², misclassified.
   → Accept as SIDEWAYS or lower penalty.

3. WHIPSAW: Rapid alternation, unreliable short segments.
   → Appropriate min_segment_length.

4. LOW R² DRIFT: Net direction but noisy path.
   → Design choice: we call it SIDEWAYS.

5. CONFLICTING SIGNALS: Features disagree.
   → Classify as SIDEWAYS.

6. SHORT SEGMENTS: Statistically unreliable.
   → Interpret with caution.

7. MISSED CHANGEPOINTS: Regime change within segment.
   → Lower penalty or better signal.

8. BOUNDARY EFFECTS: Changes near data start/end.
   → Extend data period.

KEY LESSON:

No regime detection method is perfect. Understanding failure modes
enables appropriate use and interpretation of results.

                                  CHAPTER 8
                  IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS AND DESIGN DECISIONS
================================================================================

8.1 INTRODUCTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This chapter documents our specific implementation: design decisions,
known limitations, parameter choices, and guidance for users.


8.2 ARCHITECTURE OVERVIEW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Our implementation has these components:

1. DATA INPUT: DataFrame with OHLCV columns and DatetimeIndex.

2. PELT CHANGEPOINT DETECTION:
   - Library: ruptures (Python)
   - Signal: [log_returns, rolling_volatility]
   - Kernel: RBF
   - Penalty: np.log(n) by default

3. FEATURE EXTRACTION:
   - Per-segment: OLS slope, R²
   - Per-bar: rolling mean return, rolling std return
   - Features assigned to bars based on their segment

4. GMM CLUSTERING:
   - Library: sklearn.mixture.GaussianMixture
   - Features: [slope, r_squared, mean_return, std_return]
   - Clusters: K=3 (configurable)
   - Covariance: full

5. REGIME LABELING:
   - Interpret cluster centers to assign BULL/BEAR/SIDEWAYS
   - Majority vote per segment
   - Override for conflicting signals

6. TRADE ANALYSIS:
   - Group trades by regime at entry time
   - Calculate metrics per regime
   - Assess dependency via CV


8.3 KEY DESIGN DECISIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DECISION 1: PELT vs HMM

   Choice: PELT
   Rationale:
   - Explicit segment boundaries are more interpretable
   - O(T) complexity vs O(K²T) per EM iteration
   - Simpler implementation and debugging
   - Minimum segment length is directly enforced

DECISION 2: SIGNAL FOR PELT

   Choice: [log_returns, rolling_volatility]
   Rationale (at design time):
   - Captures return behavior and volatility
   - Both are important for regime characterization

   Known limitation:
   - Primarily detects volatility changes, not trend changes
   - V-shape problem: see Chapter 7

DECISION 3: CLUSTER BARS, NOT SEGMENTS

   Choice: GMM on bars with majority vote per segment
   Rationale:
   - Ensures sufficient data points for GMM regardless of segment count
   - Avoids overfitting when there are few segments

DECISION 4: THREE REGIMES

   Choice: K=3 (BULL, BEAR, SIDEWAYS)
   Rationale:
   - Natural interpretation for trend regimes
   - Sufficient granularity for strategy analysis
   - More regimes would reduce interpretability

DECISION 5: DYNAMIC THRESHOLDS FOR WEAK TREND

   Choice: CV + percentile based thresholds
   Rationale:
   - No hardcoded magic numbers
   - Adapts to data distribution
   - Only applies when there's meaningful variation (CV > 0.2)


8.4 KNOWN LIMITATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

LIMITATION 1: V-SHAPE DETECTION

   The [log_returns, volatility] signal doesn't detect V-bottoms.
   Both drop and recovery have high volatility, so no changepoint.

   Impact: V-shaped corrections may be labeled as single segment.
   Workaround: Use longer time periods; V-shapes average out.

LIMITATION 2: LOOK-AHEAD BIAS

   Both PELT and GMM use the full dataset.
   Regime labels at time t depend on data after t.

   Impact: Cannot use labels for live trading decisions.
   Appropriate use: Post-hoc analysis only.

LIMITATION 3: MINIMUM SEGMENT LENGTH

   Fixed at 20 bars. Shorter regimes cannot be detected.

   Impact: Rapid regime changes may be missed.
   Workaround: Reduce min_segment_length (may increase noise).

LIMITATION 4: REGIME TRANSITIONS

   We assign hard labels to each bar.
   No representation of "transitioning" states.

   Impact: Bars at regime boundaries have uncertain labels.
   Workaround: Interpret boundaries as fuzzy, not sharp.


8.5 PARAMETER TUNING GUIDANCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PARAMETER: penalty (PELT)

   Default: np.log(n)
   Lower (e.g., 5): More segments, more sensitive
   Higher (e.g., 15): Fewer segments, only major changes

   Tuning: Start with default. If missing obvious regime changes, lower.
   If getting too many noisy segments, raise.

PARAMETER: min_segment_length

   Default: 20 bars
   Lower (e.g., 10): Allows shorter regimes
   Higher (e.g., 50): Only long regimes

   Tuning: Based on your trading timeframe. Day traders might want 10.
   Position traders might want 50.

PARAMETER: vol_window

   Default: 20 bars
   Purpose: Rolling window for volatility calculation in PELT signal.

   Tuning: Usually leave at 20 (one trading month).

PARAMETER: local_window

   Default: 10 bars
   Purpose: Rolling window for local mean/std features.

   Tuning: Smaller (5) for more reactive features.
   Larger (20) for smoother features.

PARAMETER: n_clusters (GMM)

   Default: 3
   Usually don't change: 3 regimes is standard for trend analysis.

PARAMETER: cv_threshold

   Default: 0.2
   Purpose: Minimum CV to apply dynamic weak-trend thresholds.

   Tuning: Lower (0.15) makes weak-trend detection more aggressive.
   Higher (0.3) makes it more conservative.

PARAMETER: weak_percentile

   Default: 25
   Purpose: Percentile for identifying weak values.

   Tuning: Higher (30) labels more segments as weak.
   Lower (20) labels fewer.


8.6 INTERPRETATION OF OUTPUTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OUTPUT: Segments

   List of Segment objects with:
   - start_date, end_date: Segment boundaries
   - regime: BULL, BEAR, or SIDEWAYS
   - bar_count: Number of bars
   - features: slope, r_squared, mean_return, std_return

   Interpretation:
   - Large segments are more reliable
   - Check features match the regime label
   - Low R² segments have less confident labels

OUTPUT: Regime Labels (per bar)

   Series indexed by date with regime at each bar.

   Interpretation:
   - Use for grouping trades by entry regime
   - Boundaries between regimes are fuzzy
   - Don't over-interpret single-bar regime at regime changes

OUTPUT: Regime Metrics (per regime)

   Dictionary with metrics for each regime:
   - total_trades: Number of trades entered in this regime
   - total_return_pct: Sum of trade returns
   - win_rate: Percentage of winning trades
   - avg_pnl_pct: Average trade P&L
   - sharpe_ratio: Simplified Sharpe

   Interpretation:
   - Compare across regimes
   - Small trade counts have unreliable metrics
   - Large differences suggest regime dependency

OUTPUT: Dependency Assessment

   - dependency_level: INDEPENDENT, MODERATE, or DEPENDENT
   - dependency_score: CV of returns across regimes

   Interpretation:
   - INDEPENDENT (CV < 0.3): Strategy works similarly across regimes
   - MODERATE (0.3-0.6): Some regime preference
   - DEPENDENT (CV > 0.6): Strong regime dependency - caution!


8.7 EXTENDING FOR NEW REGIME TYPES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The architecture supports extension to other regime types.

SESSION REGIMES (future):

   For crypto markets that trade 24/7:
   - ASIA_SESSION, EU_SESSION, US_SESSION, OVERLAP

   Implementation:
   - No changepoint detection needed
   - Classify by time of day
   - Analyze trades by session

EVENT REGIMES (future):

   User-labeled periods:
   - COVID, WAR, EARNINGS, etc.

   Implementation:
   - Accept user-provided date ranges
   - Override detected regimes with user labels
   - Analyze trades by event type

VOLATILITY REGIMES (enhancement):

   Add separate volatility classification:
   - LOW_VOL, NORMAL_VOL, HIGH_VOL

   Implementation:
   - Use percentile of rolling volatility
   - Label each bar with both trend AND volatility regime
   - Enable 2D regime analysis (e.g., BULL + HIGH_VOL)


8.8 CHAPTER SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

KEY POINTS:

1. Our implementation uses PELT + GMM for interpretability and simplicity.

2. Known limitations:
   - V-shape detection (signal issue)
   - Look-ahead bias (use for post-hoc only)
   - Fixed minimum segment length

3. Parameter tuning:
   - penalty controls sensitivity
   - min_segment_length controls minimum regime duration
   - Most parameters can stay at defaults

4. Outputs should be interpreted with understanding of limitations:
   - Large segments more reliable than small
   - Regime boundaries are fuzzy
   - Dependency assessment requires sufficient trades per regime


================================================================================
                                  CHAPTER 9
                      ALTERNATIVE APPROACHES COMPARISON
================================================================================

9.1 INTRODUCTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Our PELT+GMM approach is one of many possible regime detection methods.
This chapter compares alternatives to help users understand trade-offs.


9.2 RULE-BASED APPROACHES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

APPROACH: SMA CROSSOVER

   Definition:
   - BULL: SMA(50) > SMA(200) ("golden cross")
   - BEAR: SMA(50) < SMA(200) ("death cross")
   - (Optionally) SIDEWAYS: SMAs close together

   Implementation:
   ```
   def detect_regime(price, sma50, sma200):
       if sma50 > sma200:
           return "BULL"
       else:
           return "BEAR"
   ```

   Pros:
   - Simple, fast, interpretable
   - No look-ahead bias (only uses past data)
   - Well-understood by traders

   Cons:
   - Lagging (SMAs smooth heavily)
   - Arbitrary threshold (why 50/200?)
   - No SIDEWAYS detection without additional rules
   - Many false signals in choppy markets


APPROACH: ADX-BASED

   Definition:
   - TRENDING: ADX > 25
   - SIDEWAYS: ADX < 20
   - Use +DI/-DI for direction when trending

   Implementation:
   ```
   def detect_regime(adx, plus_di, minus_di):
       if adx < 20:
           return "SIDEWAYS"
       elif plus_di > minus_di:
           return "BULL"
       else:
           return "BEAR"
   ```

   Pros:
   - Explicitly detects trending vs non-trending
   - Standard indicator available everywhere

   Cons:
   - Thresholds (20, 25) are arbitrary
   - ADX lags significantly
   - ADX can be high in both bull and bear (doesn't show direction alone)


APPROACH: PRICE VS MOVING AVERAGE

   Definition:
   - BULL: Price > SMA(200) AND price has been above for N days
   - BEAR: Price < SMA(200) AND price has been below for N days
   - SIDEWAYS: Crossing frequently

   Pros:
   - Intuitive (price above/below trend)
   - Easy to implement

   Cons:
   - Defining "crossing frequently" is subjective
   - Threshold N is arbitrary


9.3 THRESHOLD-BASED CLASSIFICATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

APPROACH: ROLLING RETURN THRESHOLDS

   Definition based on 60-day rolling returns:
   - BULL: rolling_return > +5%
   - BEAR: rolling_return < -5%
   - SIDEWAYS: between -5% and +5%

   Pros:
   - Directly measures what we care about (returns)
   - Easy to backtest

   Cons:
   - Thresholds are arbitrary
   - Different assets need different thresholds
   - Rapid regime changes in volatile markets

APPROACH: VOLATILITY PERCENTILE

   Definition:
   - LOW_VOL: volatility < 25th percentile
   - HIGH_VOL: volatility > 75th percentile
   - NORMAL_VOL: in between

   Pros:
   - Adapts to asset's typical volatility
   - No fixed threshold

   Cons:
   - Requires historical data for percentiles
   - Doesn't detect trend direction


9.4 MARKOV-SWITCHING MODELS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

APPROACH: MARKOV-SWITCHING REGRESSION

   Model:
   - Returns depend on hidden regime: r_t = μ_{s_t} + ε_{s_t}
   - Regime follows Markov chain: P(s_t | s_{t-1})

   Estimation:
   - Maximum likelihood via EM (similar to HMM)
   - Hamilton filter for online inference

   Pros:
   - Principled probabilistic framework
   - Estimates transition probabilities
   - Regime persistence modeled explicitly

   Cons:
   - Computationally intensive
   - Requires specifying number of regimes
   - Sensitive to initialization


APPROACH: THRESHOLD AUTOREGRESSION (TAR)

   Model:
   - Different AR model depending on threshold variable
   - r_t = φ_1 · r_{t-1} + ε_t  if z_t < c
   - r_t = φ_2 · r_{t-1} + ε_t  if z_t ≥ c

   Pros:
   - Regime depends on observable variable z_t
   - No hidden states

   Cons:
   - Must choose threshold variable
   - Only two regimes (without extension)


9.5 DEEP LEARNING APPROACHES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

APPROACH: LSTM CLASSIFIER

   Model:
   - Feed sequence of returns to LSTM
   - Output: regime probabilities

   Training:
   - Requires labeled data (problematic - where do labels come from?)
   - Or use pseudo-labels from another method

   Pros:
   - Can learn complex patterns
   - Handles long-range dependencies

   Cons:
   - Needs lots of training data
   - Black box (not interpretable)
   - Prone to overfitting
   - Label generation is circular


APPROACH: TRANSFORMER-BASED

   Similar to LSTM but with attention mechanism.

   Pros:
   - State-of-the-art for sequence modeling
   - Can attend to relevant past information

   Cons:
   - Same as LSTM plus higher computational cost
   - Overkill for this problem


9.6 COMPARISON MATRIX
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Method              | Complexity | Interpretable | Look-ahead | Adapts to Data
────────────────────┼────────────┼───────────────┼────────────┼───────────────
SMA Crossover       | Low        | High          | No         | No
ADX-Based           | Low        | Medium        | No         | No
Return Threshold    | Low        | High          | No         | No
PELT + GMM          | Medium     | Medium        | Yes        | Yes
HMM                 | Medium     | Medium        | Yes*       | Yes
Markov-Switching    | High       | Low           | No         | Yes
LSTM/Transformer    | High       | Low           | No         | Yes

* HMM can be used with filtering (no look-ahead) or smoothing (look-ahead)


Method              | Handles V-shape | Handles Volatility | # Regimes
────────────────────┼─────────────────┼────────────────────┼──────────
SMA Crossover       | Yes (lagged)    | No                 | 2
ADX-Based           | Somewhat        | Implicit           | 2-3
Return Threshold    | Yes             | No                 | 3
PELT + GMM          | No (our signal) | Yes (in signal)    | K
HMM                 | Yes             | Yes                | K
Markov-Switching    | Yes             | Yes                | K
LSTM/Transformer    | Yes             | Yes                | K


9.7 RECOMMENDATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

FOR QUICK ANALYSIS:
   Use SMA crossover or ADX. Fast, no training, good enough for overview.

FOR BACKTESTING ANALYSIS:
   Use PELT+GMM (our method) or HMM. Adapts to data, provides segments.

FOR LIVE TRADING SIGNALS:
   Use rule-based (SMA, ADX) to avoid look-ahead bias.
   Or use HMM with filtering (not smoothing).

FOR RESEARCH:
   Compare multiple methods. Use robust method (Markov-switching) as benchmark.

AVOID:
   Deep learning for regime detection. Overkill, needs labels, not interpretable.


9.8 CHAPTER SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

KEY POINTS:

1. Rule-based methods (SMA, ADX) are simple and lag-free but arbitrary.

2. PELT+GMM and HMM adapt to data but have look-ahead bias.

3. Markov-switching models are principled but complex.

4. Deep learning is overkill and has label circularity problem.

5. No method is universally best. Choose based on:
   - Use case (analysis vs live trading)
   - Interpretability needs
   - Computational resources
   - Tolerance for look-ahead bias


================================================================================
                                  CHAPTER 10
               USING REGIME DETECTION FOR STRATEGY ANALYSIS
================================================================================

10.1 INTRODUCTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This final chapter connects regime detection to its primary use case:
understanding how trading strategies perform across market conditions.


10.2 WHY ANALYZE STRATEGIES BY REGIME?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SCENARIO:

   You backtest a strategy. It shows +50% return over 5 years.
   Should you trade it live?

WITHOUT REGIME ANALYSIS:

   "Great, +50% return, let's do it!"

WITH REGIME ANALYSIS:

   BULL:     +80% (3 years of bull market)
   BEAR:     -40% (1 year of bear market)
   SIDEWAYS: +10% (1 year of choppy market)

   Now you see: The strategy LOST money in bear markets.
   The +50% total came entirely from bull markets.

CRITICAL QUESTION:

   "Will the future have bear markets?"

   If yes, this strategy will likely underperform expectations.


10.3 REGIME DEPENDENCY ASSESSMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

COEFFICIENT OF VARIATION (CV) APPROACH:

We measure regime dependency using CV of returns across regimes:

   CV = std(regime_returns) / |mean(regime_returns)|

INTERPRETATION:

   CV < 0.3:  INDEPENDENT
              Returns similar across regimes.
              Strategy works in all conditions.

   CV 0.3-0.6: MODERATE
               Some preference for certain regimes.
               Investigate underperforming regimes.

   CV > 0.6:  DEPENDENT
              Highly regime-dependent.
              May fail spectacularly in wrong regime.

EXAMPLE 10.1

   BULL return:     +25%
   BEAR return:     +20%
   SIDEWAYS return: +22%

   Mean: 22.3%, Std: 2.5%
   CV = 2.5 / 22.3 = 0.11 → INDEPENDENT

EXAMPLE 10.2

   BULL return:     +40%
   BEAR return:     -15%
   SIDEWAYS return: +5%

   Mean: 10%, Std: 27.8%
   CV = 27.8 / 10 = 2.78 → DEPENDENT (capped at 1.0 in practice)


10.4 INTERPRETING REGIME METRICS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

KEY METRICS PER REGIME:

1. TOTAL TRADES: How often the strategy trades in this regime.

   - Low trades in BEAR: Strategy avoids bear markets (possibly good)
   - Low trades in BULL: Strategy misses bull markets (possibly bad)

2. TOTAL RETURN: Cumulative P&L from trades in this regime.

   - Positive in all regimes: Strategy is robust
   - Negative in any regime: Strategy fails in that condition

3. WIN RATE: Percentage of winning trades.

   - Compare across regimes
   - Large drop in one regime indicates vulnerability

4. SHARPE RATIO: Risk-adjusted return.

   - Negative Sharpe in any regime is a red flag
   - Compare to see where strategy is most efficient


10.5 RISK FLAGS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Our implementation generates automatic risk flags:

FLAG: "Strategy loses money in BEAR markets (-X%)"

   Triggered when: BEAR return < -10%

   Implication: Strategy will suffer in next bear market.
   Consider: Stop trading during bear regimes (but how to detect in real-time?)

FLAG: "Win rate varies significantly: X% (BULL) vs Y% (BEAR)"

   Triggered when: Win rate difference > 20 percentage points

   Implication: Strategy's edge is regime-dependent.
   Consider: Different position sizing by regime.

FLAG: "High variance in performance across regimes"

   Triggered when: CV > 0.6 (DEPENDENT level)

   Implication: Strategy has strong regime preference.
   Consider: Develop separate strategies for each regime.


10.6 ACTIONABLE RECOMMENDATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

RECOMMENDATION FOR INDEPENDENT STRATEGIES:

   "Strategy performs consistently across market regimes.
    Results suggest the strategy is not dependent on specific
    market conditions."

   Action: Can trade with confidence across regimes.

RECOMMENDATION FOR MODERATE STRATEGIES:

   "Strategy shows some variation across market regimes.
    Consider investigating performance in underperforming regimes.
    May benefit from regime-specific position sizing."

   Actions:
   - Analyze WHY it underperforms in certain regimes
   - Consider reducing position size in weak regimes
   - May not need major changes

RECOMMENDATION FOR DEPENDENT STRATEGIES:

   "Strategy is highly dependent on market regime.
    Performs best in BULL markets.
    Consider adding regime filters or developing separate
    strategies for each regime."

   Actions:
   - DO NOT trade without regime awareness
   - Either: Add regime filter (only trade in favorable regime)
   - Or: Develop regime-specific variants
   - Or: Combine with regime-opposite strategy


10.7 REGIME-FILTERED BACKTESTING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CONCEPT:

   Run backtest, but only take trades in certain regimes.

IMPLEMENTATION:

   1. Detect regimes for entire period
   2. Run backtest normally
   3. Filter out trades that entered during unfavorable regimes
   4. Recalculate metrics on filtered trades

EXAMPLE 10.3

   Original strategy: +50% return, 100 trades

   After filtering (BULL only):
   - Trades in BULL: 60 (60% of trades)
   - Return from BULL trades: +70%
   - Return if only trading BULL: +70%

   The filtered strategy is more profitable!
   But: You need a way to detect BULL in real-time.

CAUTION:

   Regime filtering in backtests uses future information.
   In live trading, you won't know the regime with certainty.
   Build in buffer/delay for regime detection.


10.8 POSITION SIZING BY REGIME
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CONCEPT:

   Adjust position size based on current regime.

APPROACH:

   1. Calculate strategy's Sharpe ratio per regime
   2. Scale position size proportionally

   position_size = base_size × (regime_sharpe / overall_sharpe)

EXAMPLE 10.4

   Overall Sharpe: 1.0
   BULL Sharpe: 1.5
   BEAR Sharpe: 0.3
   SIDEWAYS Sharpe: 0.8

   Position sizes:
   - BULL: 100% × (1.5 / 1.0) = 150% (increase)
   - BEAR: 100% × (0.3 / 1.0) = 30% (reduce)
   - SIDEWAYS: 100% × (0.8 / 1.0) = 80%

BENEFIT:

   Reduces exposure during unfavorable regimes.
   Increases exposure during favorable regimes.
   Improves overall risk-adjusted returns.

CAUTION:

   Still requires real-time regime detection.
   Consider using lagging indicator (SMA crossover) for live sizing.


10.9 COMBINING WITH OTHER ROBUSTNESS ANALYSES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

REGIME DETECTION + PARAMETER SENSITIVITY:

   Run parameter sensitivity analysis per regime.

   You might find:
   - Strategy is robust in BULL (parameters don't matter)
   - Strategy is fragile in BEAR (small parameter change kills it)

   This reveals WHERE the strategy is overfit.

REGIME DETECTION + WALK-FORWARD:

   Check if walk-forward consistency varies by regime.

   You might find:
   - BULL windows: Consistent performance
   - BEAR windows: Inconsistent performance

   This reveals WHEN the strategy breaks down.

PROPOSED: ROBUSTNESS CONFIDENCE SCORE

   Combine multiple analyses:

   Score = w1 × parameter_stability +
           w2 × walk_forward_consistency +
           w3 × (1 - regime_dependency)

   Score 80-100: LOW risk (robust)
   Score 60-79:  MODERATE risk
   Score 0-59:   HIGH risk (fragile)


10.10 CHAPTER SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

KEY POINTS:

1. Regime analysis reveals hidden strategy vulnerabilities.

2. CV-based dependency assessment:
   - INDEPENDENT: Works across regimes
   - MODERATE: Some regime preference
   - DEPENDENT: High risk in wrong regime

3. Risk flags identify specific problems:
   - Losses in certain regimes
   - Win rate variation
   - High performance variance

4. Actions for dependent strategies:
   - Regime filtering (trade only favorable regimes)
   - Regime-based position sizing
   - Develop regime-specific strategies

5. Combine with parameter sensitivity and walk-forward for complete picture.
